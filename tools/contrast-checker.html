WCAG Standards

      

        
AA Normal Text: Minimum ratio 4.5:1

        
AA Large Text (18px+ bold or 24px+): Minimum ratio 3:1

        
AAA Normal Text: Minimum ratio 7:1

        
AAA Large Text: Minimum ratio 4.5:1

        
Non-text UI components & graphics: Minimum ratio 3:1

      

    

    
    

      
What a contrast ratio actually measures

      
The number is a ratio of relative luminance — how much light each colour reflects — not a difference in hue. It runs from 1:1, where the two colours are identical, to 21:1 for pure black on pure white. Nothing can exceed 21:1.

      
Because it ignores hue, two colours can look very different and still fail. Red text on a green background is a classic example: the colours are opposite, the luminance is similar, and the ratio can fall below 3:1. Conversely, two shades of the same blue can pass easily if one is much darker than the other.

      
This is also why a colour-blind simulation is a separate check. Contrast tells you whether there is enough light difference; simulation tells you whether the hue difference survives for someone who does not distinguish those hues. A design can pass one and fail the other.


      
The thresholds, and why they differ

      

        
RequirementRatioApplies to

        
AA normal text4.5:1Body copy, labels, most interface text

        
AA large text3:124px and above, or 18.66px bold

        
AAA normal text7:1Enhanced level, often required in government work

        
AAA large text4.5:1Headings at the enhanced level

        
Non-text contrast3:1Input borders, icons, focus rings, chart lines

      

      
Large text gets a lower threshold because thicker strokes stay legible with less luminance difference. The cut-off is a real boundary: 23px regular text needs 4.5:1, 24px needs only 3:1, so one pixel of font size can change whether a colour passes.


      
The mistake that defeats most checks

      
People test text against the colour they think is behind it, rather than the colour actually painted there. Two things break that assumption:

      
    
        
  • Translucency. A panel at 10% white over a dark page is not white — it is a blend. Testing the text against pure white gives a number that has nothing to do with what is on screen.
    • 
        
  • Stacking. A badge inside a card inside a section may have three translucent layers between the text and the page background. The effective colour is all of them composited, in order.
    • 
      

      
The same trap applies to gradients. A button filled with a gradient has no single background colour, so the honest test is against its darkest stop when the text is light — because that stop is where the ratio is worst and where the text will be hardest to read.


      
Fills and text need different colours

      
A colour chosen to be readable as text on a dark background is light. Use that same colour as a fill behind white text and the requirement inverts — now it needs to be dark. This is the most common reason a status colour that reads fine in one place becomes unreadable in another.

      
In practice you want two variants of each semantic colour: one tuned as text against your background, one tuned as a fill with light text on top. Trying to make a single value serve both roles usually produces a colour that is mediocre at each.


      
What a ratio does not tell you

      
Passing 4.5:1 is a floor, not a design goal. Several things matter that the number cannot see:

      
    
        
  • Font weight. A thin 300-weight face at 4.5:1 is harder to read than a 500-weight at the same ratio, because there is less ink.
    • 
        
  • Size. The threshold drops at 24px, but 12px at 4.6:1 is still uncomfortable for sustained reading.
    • 
        
  • Surrounding light. A phone in sunlight loses effective contrast; a dim room makes bright text glare.
    • 
        
  • Meaning carried by colour alone. A red field with no icon or message fails for a colour-blind user regardless of its ratio.
    • 
      


      
Fixing a failure without redesigning

      
When a pair fails, you usually have more room than you think:

      
    
        
  1. Darken or lighten one side only. Moving both toward the middle reduces the ratio; moving one away from the other increases it.
    2. 
        
  2. Keep the hue, change the lightness. This preserves the brand feel while fixing the number, which is usually an easier conversation than changing the colour.
    3. 
        
  3. Give the text its own surface. A message on a solid panel is independent of whatever is behind the panel, so it cannot be broken by a later change elsewhere.
    4. 
        
  4. Raise the size or weight. If the text can legitimately be 24px or bold, the threshold drops to 3:1 and a colour that failed may now pass.

About This Tool
Check if your text and background color combination meets WCAG accessibility standards. Enter any two colors and instantly see the contrast ratio with pass/fail results for AA and AAA levels (both normal and large text). Includes a live preview showing how your text looks on the background, color swap button, and HEX input. Essential for web designers and developers building accessible websites that everyone can read, including people with visual impairments.


Frequently Asked Questions


Is this tool really free?
Yes. Color Contrast Checker has no charge, no sign-up and no paid tier. The .txt/.csv output carries no watermark and there is no cap on how often you use it. Color Contrast Checker keeps its state in your own browser storage, so there is no account to fund and the site runs on advertising instead.


Is my data safe?
Yes. Color Contrast Checker works entirely in the page. Everything Color Contrast Checker remembers is under the ccPairs key in your browser, which no server can read. Anything remembered between visits sits in local storage under ccPairs, which is on your device and invisible to us. Clearing browser data clears it.


Does it work on mobile?
Yes. Color Contrast Checker runs in the browser rather than as an installed application, so a phone, a tablet and a desktop behave the same.


Does it work offline?
Yes, after the first visit. Color Contrast Checker is cached by a service worker, so opening it again works with no connection. What you saved is under ccPairs in this browser, which is readable with no connection.


Can I use this for commercial purposes?
Yes. Anything Color Contrast Checker produces is yours for any purpose, personal or commercial, with no attribution required. The .csv Color Contrast Checker writes contains only what you entered. The .csv, .txt file Color Contrast Checker produces is written on your machine and never passes through ours, so there is nothing for us to hold a claim over.
