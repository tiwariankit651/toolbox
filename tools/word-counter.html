📊 Word Counter & Readability

Paste or type text for live stats, keyword density, readability scores, and platform character limits. Everything runs in your browser.

Frequently Asked Questions

What statistics does it show as I type?

Live counts for words, characters, characters without spaces, sentences, paragraphs, lines, unique words, average word length, average sentence length, longest word, plus a reading time (200 wpm) and speaking time (130 wpm). It also highlights your single longest sentence with its word count. Everything updates on every keystroke and your text auto-saves locally under wordCounterText.

Which platform character limits are tracked?

A Character Limits panel shows a progress bar and remaining/over count against eight presets: Twitter/X (280), Instagram Caption (2200), Instagram Bio (150), Meta Description (160), Title Tag (60), SMS (160), LinkedIn Post (3000), and YouTube Description (5000). The bar turns red when you exceed a limit and tells you by how many characters.

What readability scores does it calculate?

Three: Flesch Reading Ease (with a plain-language interpretation of the score band), Flesch-Kincaid Grade Level, and the Gunning Fog Index, each with a grade-level explanation. They're derived from words-per-sentence and syllable counts. The page notes these formulas are a rough signal — they catch over-long sentences and dense vocabulary but can't judge whether the writing is actually clear.

Can it analyse keywords and phrases, and export?

Yes. It lists the top 15 keywords with count and density (with a toggle to filter common stop words) and finds repeated 2-word and 3-word phrases. You can Export Report as a .txt summary (stats, readability, keywords, longest sentence) and download a word-frequency CSV. Quick case-conversion and remove-extra-spaces buttons are built in too.

What the numbers actually tell you

A word count answers a requirement. The other figures answer a more useful question — whether the writing is readable — and they are worth knowing how to interpret.

Readability scores, and their limits

Flesch-Kincaid and similar formulas count syllables per word and words per sentence, then map the result to a school grade. They are a rough signal, not a verdict.

What they catch reliably: sentences that have grown too long, and vocabulary that is denser than the audience needs. What they cannot see: whether the argument is clear, whether the structure helps, or whether a short sentence is actually ambiguous. A text can score well and be incomprehensible, because the formula never reads the words.

A practical reading: an average sentence over about 25 words is probably harder than it needs to be. Under 10 throughout reads as choppy. Variety is what reads well.

Sentence length distribution beats the average

An average of 18 words can mean every sentence is 18 words, which is monotonous, or a mix of 6 and 30, which has rhythm. The distribution is the useful figure and the average hides it.

Keyword density, and why the number is a trap

Density is occurrences divided by total words. It is worth checking for the opposite reason to the one people assume: not to hit a target, but to notice accidental repetition. Using "important" nine times in a page is something you cannot see while writing.

There is no density figure that helps search ranking. Writing to one produces text that reads as written to a number, which is what both readers and search engines are now good at detecting.

Reading time

Usually computed at 200 to 250 words per minute, which is a reasonable average for comprehension. Treat it as an order of magnitude: technical material is slower, and a reader skimming is much faster.

Counting conventions that differ

Word counts vary between tools by a few per cent, because the rules differ. Hyphenated words may count as one or two. Numbers may or may not count. A URL may count as one word or several. Footnotes and headings may be included or not.

Where a limit is strict — a journal submission, an exam answer — the count that matters is the one made by whoever is checking. If their tool says 2,010 and yours says 1,995, theirs decides.
