✂️ Crop Image

Drag a crop box over your photo, or type exact pixel X/Y/W/H. Cropping happens at full original resolution — nothing is uploaded, everything runs in your browser.

How to Use This Tool

  1. Upload a photo — drag and drop or tap to pick. iPhone HEIC files are decoded in your browser.
  2. Pick an aspect ratio (or keep it free), or tick "Custom ratio" and type your own, e.g. 35:45 for a passport photo.
  3. Drag the box to position it and drag the corner or edge handles to resize. Prefer numbers? Type exact X/Y/W/H in pixels and the box follows.
  4. Fine-tune with arrow keys — the box nudges one pixel at a time, or ten with Shift held.
  5. Choose a format (keep original, or JPG/PNG/WebP) and click Crop & Download.

Common Use Cases

  • Trimming a passport or ID photo to the exact 35×45 ratio a form demands
  • Cropping a profile picture square (1:1) for a social account
  • Cutting a 16:9 banner or 9:16 story frame out of a larger shot
  • Removing dead space, a stray finger, or a distracting edge from a photo
  • Pulling an exact pixel region out of a screenshot for a document

About This Tool

Crop any image to a precise rectangle. The crop box is drawn directly on a scaled preview with eight drag handles and a rule-of-thirds grid, but the actual crop is computed in the original image's pixels, so there is no quality loss from the preview being smaller than the file. Lock the box to a fixed aspect ratio — 1:1, 4:3, 3:4, 16:9, 9:16, 3:2, passport 35×45 or your own custom ratio — or crop freely. Type exact X/Y/W/H pixel values and the box updates, and vice versa. EXIF orientation from phone cameras is honoured so portraits are not cropped sideways, and the re-encode drops EXIF and GPS metadata from the output. Everything runs in your browser.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does cropping lose quality because the preview is smaller than my photo?

No. The crop box is drawn on a scaled-down preview so it fits your screen, but the actual crop is computed in the original image's pixels. The tool keeps a full-resolution copy of the decoded image and reads the X/Y/W/H you set against that, so a region cut from a 4000-pixel photo comes out at its true pixel size, not the preview size. The X/Y/W/H boxes always show the real pixel numbers.

Which aspect ratios can I lock the box to?

Free (any shape), 1:1, 4:3, 3:4, 16:9, 9:16, 3:2 and passport 35×45, plus a custom ratio where you type your own width and height (for example 35:45 or 2:3). When a ratio is locked, resizing a handle keeps that ratio, and typing a width in the pixel boxes recomputes the height automatically so you can't accidentally break the proportions.

Will a photo taken on my phone be cropped sideways?

No. Phone photos store an EXIF orientation tag instead of rotating the pixels, which makes some tools crop them rotated. This tool decodes the image with createImageBitmap using imageOrientation 'from-image' (falling back to the browser's own orientation handling), so the picture is upright before you crop and the region you select is the region you get.

What format and metadata does the cropped file have?

You can keep the original format or choose JPG, PNG or WebP, with a quality slider for the lossy formats. Because the crop is re-encoded from a canvas, EXIF and GPS metadata are dropped from the saved file. The output filename reports the exact pixel dimensions of the crop, and nothing is uploaded — the whole operation runs in your browser.

Cropping in original pixels, and why the preview is only a guide

Almost every browser-based cropper has the same quiet problem: the box you draw lives on a picture that has been shrunk to fit your screen. If the tool crops that on-screen picture, a 4000-pixel photo cropped on a 900-pixel preview throws away three-quarters of the detail before you even press the button. This tool avoids that entirely. The decoded image is held at full resolution, the box is translated back into the original pixel grid, and the crop is cut from the real file — so the X, Y, W and H numbers you see are the true dimensions of what you will get.

Reading and typing the pixel boxes

The four fields — X, Y, W, H — are the top-left corner and the size of the crop in original pixels. They are two-way: drag the box and the numbers update; type a number and the box jumps to match. This matters when a form is specific, for example "upload a 350×350 photo". Type 350 into W, and if an aspect ratio is locked the H fills in to keep the proportions. For a free crop you can set all four by hand and position the region to the pixel. The status line below always restates the region size against the full image size so you can sanity-check before downloading.

Aspect ratios and the passport case

The preset buttons lock the box to a shape: 1:1 for a square profile picture, 16:9 and 9:16 for banners and stories, 3:2 for classic photo proportions, and 35×45 for the passport ratio used by many ID forms. With a ratio locked, every handle keeps the shape — drag a corner and both sides move together. The custom ratio field takes any two numbers, so a form asking for an unusual proportion is still one entry away. Note a ratio fixes the shape, not the pixel count; combine it with the W field, or follow up in the Image Resizer, when a form also dictates an exact size.

Why phone photos stay upright

Pictures from a phone are usually stored in landscape with an EXIF orientation tag telling the viewer to rotate them. A cropper that ignores the tag reads the pixels sideways, and you end up selecting the wrong part of the image. Here the file is decoded with createImageBitmap using imageOrientation: 'from-image', which bakes the rotation into the pixels before anything is drawn, with a fallback to the browser's native orientation for older environments. The upshot is that a portrait selfie behaves like a portrait, and the region you select is the region that comes out.

Keyboard nudging and the rule-of-thirds grid

Once the box is roughly placed, the arrow keys move it one pixel at a time, or ten pixels with Shift held — far more precise than dragging on a small screen. The faint grid dividing the box into thirds is the photographer's rule of thirds: placing a subject or horizon on those lines usually composes better than dead centre. When you are happy, pick a format — keep the original, or convert to JPG, PNG or WebP — and the re-encode also strips EXIF and GPS data from the saved file.
