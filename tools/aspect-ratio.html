Why a ratio matters more than the pixel size

Two images at 1920×1080 and 1280×720 are different sizes but the same shape. Both are 16:9, so both fill a widescreen frame edge to edge with nothing cropped and no bars added. Change one number and the shape changes: 1920×1200 is 16:10, which looks almost identical in a list of dimensions but will be letterboxed or trimmed wherever 16:9 is expected.

This is why upload rules are usually stated as a ratio rather than a size. A platform does not care whether your thumbnail is 1280 or 1920 wide — it will scale it. It cares about the shape, because that is what it cannot fix without cutting something off.

What actually happens when the ratio is wrong

A service has three choices when the shape does not match, and each one costs you something:

  • Crop — the image is scaled until it covers the frame and the overflow is cut. Centre crops are the norm, so anything near an edge is at risk. This is how a headline gets its top sliced off or a logo loses a corner.
  • Letterbox or pillarbox — the whole image is kept and bars are added. Nothing is lost, but the image is smaller than it could be and the bars look like a mistake.
  • Stretch — rare now, but some older systems still distort rather than crop. Circles become ovals and text looks subtly wrong in a way that is hard to name.

You cannot control which one a platform picks. You can control the shape you hand it, which removes the decision.

Safe areas: the part of the frame you can rely on

Even at the correct ratio, not all of the frame is reliably visible. A video thumbnail has a duration badge in one corner. A profile banner is cropped differently on a phone than on a desktop. A document preview may round the corners.

The practical rule is to keep anything essential — faces, text, logos — inside the central area and treat the outer margin as decorative. If your composition only works when every pixel survives, it will break on the first platform that crops.

Scaling without losing the shape

To resize while keeping the ratio, you only ever need one new number. Multiply the other by the same factor: to take 1920×1080 down to 800 wide, the factor is 800 ÷ 1920 ≈ 0.4167, so the height becomes 1080 × 0.4167 ≈ 450. The Scale tab does this both ways, by target dimension or by percentage.

Rounding is where it goes wrong. 0.4167 × 1080 is 450.0 here, but many factors produce a fraction, and a pixel cannot be fractional. Rounding one side and not the other shifts the ratio very slightly — usually invisible, occasionally enough to trigger a crop on a strict uploader. Rounding both sides in the same direction keeps you closer to the original shape.

Enlarging is not the same as reducing

Making an image smaller discards information, which is safe. Making it larger has to invent information, which is not. A 400×300 image scaled to 1600×1200 has four times the pixels and no extra detail, so it looks soft. The ratio is still correct — the sharpness is not.

If you need a large image, start from a large original. The arithmetic here will give you the right shape at any size, but it cannot add detail that was never captured.
