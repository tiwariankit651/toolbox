Converting a colour PDF to grayscale for cheaper, cleaner printing

Colour toner costs several times what black toner does, and a document full of colour you do not need wastes it on every page. Converting to grayscale or black-and-white before printing fixes that, and it also makes busy, colourful pages calmer to read and to annotate. This tool does the conversion entirely in your browser, but the method it has to use shapes the result, so it is worth understanding what happens to your file.

Why the output is image-based

The PDF library that builds the new document cannot recolour existing vector content in place — there is no reliable way to reach inside an arbitrary PDF and desaturate its drawing commands. So each page is instead rendered to a canvas with pdf.js at your chosen DPI, every pixel is converted to grey, and that image becomes one page of a freshly built PDF. The consequence is honest and worth repeating: a page that was crisp vector text and line art becomes a picture of that text. It will still look right, but a text-only document often ends up larger than the colour original, which is why the tool shows the input and output sizes together after it finishes. Photo-heavy pages usually get smaller.

Grayscale versus pure black and white

Grayscale keeps every shade using the standard luminance weighting — about thirty percent red, fifty-nine percent green, eleven percent blue — which matches how the eye perceives brightness and keeps photographs and shaded diagrams looking natural. Those pages are stored as JPEG inside the PDF to keep the size down. Pure black and white instead forces each pixel to full black or full white at a threshold you move with the slider. That is ideal for text, forms and line drawings, where grey halftones only muddy the result, and it gives the lightest possible load on a mono printer. Black-and-white pages are stored as PNG so the sharp edges stay sharp.

Keeping the text searchable

Turning a page into an image would normally throw away the ability to search or copy its words. The optional "Keep text searchable" setting avoids that by reading each page's original text positions and drawing them onto the new page as a fully invisible layer sitting over the grey image. You see the picture; a find or a copy still hits the real words underneath. Because this uses the built-in Helvetica font, which only covers Latin-1 characters, anything outside that range is quietly skipped from the hidden layer — the visible image is never affected, so no content is lost on screen.

DPI, page size and rotation

DPI decides how many pixels each page is rendered at: 150 for a small draft, 200 for everyday printing, 300 for print quality and the largest files. Whichever you pick, each new page is created at the original page's displayed size and keeps its rotation, so a landscape sheet or a page turned sideways in the source comes out upright and at the correct dimensions rather than squashed or rotated. Pages are processed one at a time with a progress bar, and Cancel stops the run cleanly without producing a half-built file.
