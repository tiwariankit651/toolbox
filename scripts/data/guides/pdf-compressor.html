Hitting a Size Limit Without Ruining Legibility

How this compressor actually shrinks a file

This tool does not optimise the PDF's internal objects — it rasterises. Each page is rendered to a canvas at your chosen DPI, re-encoded as a JPEG at a chosen quality, and the pages are rebuilt into a new PDF. That approach is what produces the large reductions, but it has one unavoidable cost: the output pages are images, so text in the result is no longer selectable, searchable or copyable. Compression here is one-way and cannot be undone, so keep your original file. If you need the text to stay live, this is the wrong tool; if you just need a smaller file to clear an upload limit, it is exactly right.

Target-size mode and how it searches

When a portal demands "under 2 MB" or "under 500 KB", switch Mode to "Target size (auto-find quality)" and enter the KB limit. The tool then binary-searches the JPEG quality over up to seven attempts: it tries a mid quality, and if the result fits it raises quality to preserve detail, or if it is too big it lowers quality, converging on the best quality that still lands under your limit. If even the lowest quality cannot reach the target, it says so and suggests lowering the DPI or enabling grayscale — the two levers that cut size beyond quality alone. The presets wire this up for you: Email aims under roughly 5 MB, Government portal under 2 MB, and Government portal strict under 500 KB with grayscale already on.

The settings that move the needle

Beyond quality, three controls change the output materially:

  • DPI sets the render resolution — 72 is smallest, 150 is the recommended balance, 200 is print-grade and largest. Dropping DPI shrinks the file more reliably than lowering quality, because it reduces the pixel count.
  • Grayscale converts each page using standard luminance weighting, which helps on text documents where colour adds nothing.
  • Downscale caps very large pages to a maximum pixel width, useful for oversized scans.

You can also restrict compression to a page range like 1-3,5. A live estimate probes the first page or two at your current settings and extrapolates to predict the output size before you commit, with about 2% added for PDF container overhead — treat it as a close approximation, not an exact figure, since pages vary.

Checking quality before you rely on it

The most common mistake with rasterised compression is pushing quality so low that text becomes fuzzy and a reviewer rejects it. Each result shows the original versus compressed size, the percent saved, and a before/after thumbnail of page 1 side by side — look at that comparison before downloading, and if the text looks soft, raise the DPI or quality and recompress. A green percentage means it shrank; an amber "no reduction" warns you the file was already small enough that rasterising did not help. Batch mode applies the same settings to several PDFs at once, each reported individually, downloadable singly or together as a ZIP, with a copyable report summarising every result. All processing stays in your browser, so the files are never uploaded.
