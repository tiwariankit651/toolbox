Interpreting a similarity score that never touched the internet

The crucial thing to internalise before trusting any number here is the boundary of what the tool measures. It compares only the text you paste in, or scans a single document for internal repetition. It does not crawl the web, query any academic database, or know anything about sources you have not provided. A score of 0% means "the two texts you gave me barely overlap" — it does not mean "this is original" in the sense Turnitin reports. Treat it as a draft-comparison and self-editing aid, not a clearance certificate.

How the Compare score is actually built

Compare mode runs four real algorithms and blends them into one percentage: Jaccard similarity on the word sets (weighted 25%), cosine similarity on term frequencies (25%), the longest common run of consecutive words (20%), and the average of shared 3-gram and 5-gram overlap (30%). Each is also shown separately, which is more informative than the headline number. For instance, a high longest-common-run with low Jaccard points to one copied passage inside otherwise different text, whereas high Jaccard with a short common run suggests shared vocabulary but reworded sentences. The verdict bands are fixed: under 15% Original, 15–30% Low, 30–60% Moderate, 60%+ High.

The paraphrase blind spot is real

Because every method is lexical — it counts shared words and word sequences — genuinely paraphrased text that uses different vocabulary can score low even when the ideas are lifted. The tool is upfront that catching that would require semantic analysis it does not perform. In practice this means two things: do not assume a low score proves you paraphrased ethically (you still must cite), and expect the score to be sensitive to surface edits like synonym swaps, which real detectors and human readers see through.

What Self-Check mode finds

Self-Check scans one document for internal repetition rather than comparing two. It reports three things: exact duplicate sentences, near-duplicate sentence pairs above a threshold you set between 50% and 95%, and 3-word phrases that appear three or more times. These roll up into a repetition index (duplicates weighted most heavily, then near-duplicates, then overused phrases). A few practical notes:

  • Sentence detection relies on punctuation, so text without full stops yields few or no sentences to compare — the tool warns you and falls back where it can.
  • Lowering the near-duplicate threshold surfaces looser matches; raising it toward 95% catches only near-identical pairs.
  • The repetition index is a heuristic for tightening prose before submission, not a plagiarism verdict.

Privacy and keeping a record

All processing is JavaScript in your browser; nothing is uploaded, which is the main reason to use it on sensitive drafts. Your inputs are saved locally so you do not lose them, and both modes produce a copyable, downloadable .txt report that preserves the component scores and the exact shared phrases. If you need an authoritative originality check for a graded submission, run this first to clean up accidental overlap, then rely on your institution's official tool for the final word.
