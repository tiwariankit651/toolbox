🗜️ How to Compress Photo Under 20KB, 50KB, or 100KB — Free Online

October 2026 · 10 min read

Hitting an exact file size sounds like it should be a single setting, and in most tools it is not. You get a quality slider from 1 to 100 with no indication of what any value will produce, so people drag it, check, drag it again, and eventually land somewhere near the target having lost more detail than they needed to.

It is worth understanding what actually drives file size, because then the target becomes a calculation rather than a guess.

Three things decide the file size

Only one of them is the quality slider, which is why the slider alone is a frustrating control.

  • Pixel dimensions. This dominates. Halving both width and height removes three-quarters of the pixels, and the file shrinks roughly in proportion. Going from 1000 × 1150 to 200 × 230 is a 25-fold reduction before any compression at all.
  • Image complexity. A face against a plain wall compresses far smaller than the same face against foliage, at identical settings. JPEG stores change, and a flat background has almost none.
  • Quality setting. Real, but the least predictable: quality 70 on one image is 40 KB and on another is 90 KB.

The practical consequence: resize before you compress. If you need 200 × 230 anyway, doing that first does most of the size reduction for free, and the compression you then apply can be gentle enough to keep the face sharp.

Targeting a band

Government portals usually give a range — 20–50 KB, 10–20 KB, under 100 KB — and enforce both ends. The common mistake is compressing hard to be safe and falling below the floor, where the file is rejected exactly as an oversized one is.

BandAim forWhy
20–50 KB30–40 KBClear of both edges; face stays sharp
10–20 KB14–18 KBSignatures break up near the floor
Under 100 KB60–80 KBRoom for a portal re-encode
Under 20 KB15–18 KBThe tightest common case

The re-encode point is worth knowing. Some portals process what you upload and store their own version, which can shift the size by a few kilobytes either way. A file sitting at 49 KB in a 50 KB ceiling can come out the other side over the limit.

Photographs and signatures need opposite treatment

JPEG was designed for photographs: areas of gradually changing colour. It handles a face well and handles line art badly.

A signature is line art — hard black strokes on flat white, with a sharp edge at every boundary. Compressed hard, JPEG puts grey halos and speckles around every stroke, and a thin stroke can break into a dotted line. The signature becomes visibly damaged at a file size where a face would still look fine.

Two adjustments fix this:

  • Convert to greyscale first. A colour scan of black ink carries three channels describing what is essentially one. Dropping the other two shrinks the file substantially with no visible loss, which buys you a gentler compression setting.
  • Stay towards the top of the band. 14–18 KB in a 10–20 KB range, not 10–11 KB.

What over-compression actually looks like

Knowing the symptoms lets you catch it before uploading.

  • Blocking — 8 × 8 pixel squares visible in smooth areas such as a cheek or a plain wall. JPEG works in blocks of that size, and when there is not enough data each block becomes flat.
  • Ringing — faint ripples along high-contrast edges: a collar against a background, the frame of glasses.
  • Colour bleeding — colour smearing beyond its boundary, most visible on lips and on a coloured shirt collar.
  • Broken strokes — on a signature, the stroke stops being continuous.

Check at 100% zoom on your own screen. If you can see any of these, you have compressed past what the image can carry at those dimensions — resize smaller or accept a larger file.

When compression cannot reach the target

Sometimes the band is simply incompatible with the dimensions. A 1000 × 1150 photograph forced under 20 KB will look bad no matter how you compress it, because there are too many pixels for that budget.

The answer is to reduce the dimensions. If the specification allows 200 × 230, use it — that is 46,000 pixels rather than 1.15 million, and 20 KB is generous for it. People often keep a larger image because "bigger is better" and then destroy it with compression, when the smaller image would have been clean.

Format matters less than people think, with one exception

For photographs, JPEG is correct and is what portals ask for. PNG is lossless, which means a photograph saved as PNG is far larger — often ten times — and cannot be brought into a small band without converting to JPEG anyway.

The exception is the signature. PNG handles line art well and would be the better format technically. But if the portal specifies JPEG, use JPEG — a file in an unexpected format may be accepted at upload and then fail somewhere you cannot see. Follow the stated format and compensate with greyscale.

WebP compresses better than JPEG at the same quality, but government portals almost never accept it. Do not convert to WebP for a form.

A reliable sequence

  1. Start from the original, not a WhatsApp copy or a screenshot.
  2. Crop to the required ratio so nothing is stretched later.
  3. Resize to the specified pixels. This does most of the size reduction.
  4. Convert a signature to greyscale.
  5. Compress towards the middle of the band.
  6. Verify the real file size in your file manager.
  7. Inspect at 100% for blocking or broken strokes.

A note on privacy

Most free compression sites upload your image to a server, compress it there and return it. For an ordinary photo that is a minor consideration. For a photograph of your face and an image of your signature — which together are what an identity check relies on — it is worth avoiding.
