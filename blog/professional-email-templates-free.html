📧 Professional Email Templates

October 2026 · 9 min read

A professional email is judged in about three seconds, and almost all of that judgement comes from the subject line and the first sentence. Everything after that is read by someone who has already decided whether this is worth their attention.

That is the useful frame for writing one: the structure exists to get a decision from a busy person, not to be polite at length.

The subject line does most of the work

A subject line should let someone decide, without opening the message, what it is about and whether it needs them.

Three rules that cover most cases: name the specific thing, include a date or reference if one exists, and keep it under about fifty characters so it is not truncated on a phone.

The four-part structure

Nearly every professional email that works has the same shape.

  1. Why you are writing — one sentence, first sentence. "I am writing to request leave for three days in March."
  2. The context they need — only what is necessary to act. Two or three sentences at most.
  3. What you want them to do — explicit, and ideally one thing. "Could you confirm by Friday?"
  4. Close — a line, not a paragraph.

The commonest failure is putting part one last: three paragraphs of background and then the request. A busy reader has already skimmed to the bottom looking for what you want, so put it where they are looking.

Openings to avoid, and what to use

"I hope this email finds you well." Everyone writes it, nobody reads it, and it delays your first sentence. Harmless but wasted.

"Sorry to bother you." Apologising for writing frames your own message as an imposition, which is not the impression you want.

"As per my last email." Reads as an accusation whether you meant it or not. "Following up on my note of 8 October" says the same thing neutrally.

What works instead is simply starting: "I am writing about X." It is direct, which busy people read as respectful rather than brusque.

Indian professional conventions

Indian office email has its own register, and knowing which parts are standard and which are dated is worth something.

  • "Respected Sir/Madam" — standard and expected in government and academic correspondence. For a private company, "Dear Mr Sharma" is more usual.
  • "Do the needful" — understood in Indian offices, reads as dated elsewhere. Depends entirely on the recipient.
  • "Revert back" — genuinely redundant, since revert already means to go back. "Reply by Friday" is clearer.
  • "Kindly" — fine in moderation. Three kindlies in one email reads as pleading.
  • "PFA" / "PFB" — please find attached and below. Common internally; spell them out externally.

The underlying question is always who is reading. A formal register is correct for a government office and over-formal for a startup.

The common situations

Leave request

Give the dates, the reason briefly, what happens to your work, and who is covering. The last part is what makes it easy to approve: "Rohit has agreed to handle the Tuesday review."

Following up with no reply

Wait a working week unless it is genuinely time-critical. Then forward your own earlier message with one line above it: "Following up on this — is there anything you need from me?" Quoting the original saves them searching, and "anything you need from me" gives them an easy reply.

Declining something

Decline in the first sentence, give one reason, and offer an alternative if there is one. Burying a no in three paragraphs of apology leaves the reader unsure whether it was a no.

Reporting a problem

What happened, when, what the effect is, what you have already tried, and what you need. In that order. Most problem emails omit "what I have already tried", which is the part that saves a round trip.

Asking for something from a stranger

Say who you are in one line, how you found them, what specifically you are asking, and why you are asking them rather than anyone. Keep the whole thing under 150 words — a long cold email is deleted on sight.

Attachments and CC

Two habits that prevent a surprising amount of friction.

Attach before you write the body. The forgotten attachment is the most common email error and the most easily avoided — attach first, then write, and it cannot happen.

Name the file properly. Ankit-Tiwari-Resume-Oct2026.pdf rather than resume_final_v3.pdf. The recipient is saving it into a folder with twenty others.

Use CC sparingly. Everyone in CC has to decide whether the message needs them, which is a small cost multiplied by the number of people. If someone only needs to know the outcome, tell them the outcome later.

Before sending

  1. Read the first sentence alone. Does it say why you are writing?
  2. Find the ask. If you cannot locate it quickly, neither can they.
  3. Check the name spelling. Getting it wrong undoes a careful email.
  4. Read it as the recipient. Anything that could be read as blame, usually can be.
  5. Confirm the attachment is there.

On templates and privacy

A template is a structure, not a script. Sending something that reads as filled-in is worse than a plainly written original, because the reader can tell.
