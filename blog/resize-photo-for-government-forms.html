📐 How to Resize Photo for Government Forms

October 2026 · 10 min read

Every recruitment portal states its own photograph specification, and the numbers differ enough that a file prepared for one exam will often be refused by another. The underlying skill is the same in all of them, though, and it comes down to doing three operations in the right order: crop to the ratio, resize to the pixels, compress to the size band.

Do them in that order and the result is clean at any specification. Do them in a different order and you get either a stretched face or a blocky one.

Why the order matters

Each operation throws away information, and throwing it away in the wrong sequence wastes the detail you needed.

  • Crop first because it decides the shape. Cropping after resizing means you resized pixels you then deleted, so the remaining image has less detail than it could have.
  • Resize second because it sets the pixel dimensions the portal asked for. Resizing after compressing scales up the compression artefacts along with the picture.
  • Compress last because it is the only step that targets file size, and file size depends on the final pixel dimensions.

A concrete example. Take a 3000 × 4000 camera photograph that needs to become 200 × 230 at 30 KB. Crop to roughly 0.87 proportion first — say 3000 × 3450 — then resize that to 200 × 230, then compress. The face stays sharp. Compress the original to 30 KB first and you have a 3000 × 4000 image full of blocking, which resizing then shrinks but cannot repair.

Ratio, not just size

This is the step most people skip, and it is why so many admit cards show slightly stretched faces.

A specification of 200 × 230 pixels implies a ratio of about 0.87 — a little taller than wide. If your source is a square crop, or a 4:3 landscape frame, forcing it into 200 × 230 squashes or stretches the face. Nothing at upload will catch it.

200 × 230 px0.87Slightly taller than wide
3.5 × 4.5 cm print0.78Noticeably taller — not the same

Square avatar1.0Never correct for a form photo

If a tool offers you "fit" or "fill", choose fill with the crop centred on the face. Fit preserves the original ratio by adding white bars, and bars are not acceptable on a form photograph.

Reading a size band correctly

Bands such as 20–50 KB or 10–20 KB are enforced at both ends. Candidates routinely over-compress "to be safe" and land below the floor, where the file is rejected just as firmly as an oversized one.

Aim for the middle. In a 20–50 KB band, 30–40 KB is comfortable: far from both edges, and high enough that the face stays clear. There is a second reason beyond the band itself — some portals re-encode the file after upload, and a re-encode can move the size by a few kilobytes. A file at 48 KB can emerge above the ceiling.

Start from the right source

Everything downstream depends on this, and it is where most poor results begin.

  • Good: the original file from a phone or camera, typically 1–5 MB. Plenty of detail to discard.
  • Usable: a photograph a studio emailed you, if it is not already heavily compressed.
  • Poor: anything received over WhatsApp, which recompresses on send. You would be compressing an already-compressed file, and at 30 KB the blocking shows.
  • Unusable: a screenshot of a photograph, which has your screen's resolution rather than the photograph's; a crop from a group photo, which has too few pixels on the face; or a photo enlarged from something small, which invents detail and looks soft.

If the only copy you have arrived on WhatsApp, ask for it again by email or a file-sharing link. The difference at these file sizes is clearly visible.

Composition rules that apply almost everywhere

Portals differ on numbers but agree on what the photograph must show, because its purpose is identification at the centre.

  • Recent — usually within three months.
  • Plain light background — white or near-white, no pattern, no doorway.
  • Face forward, both eyes visible, neutral expression.
  • Even lighting — the most common quality failure. Face a window; do not stand with your back to one.
  • No cap or sunglasses. Prescription glasses are generally fine if there is no glare across the eyes.
  • Head and upper shoulders in frame, with a small margin above the hair.

A plain bedsheet in daylight, photographed by someone else from about a metre away, satisfies all of this. An arm's-length selfie usually fails on angle and distance.

Checking the result properly

Two checks take thirty seconds and catch nearly everything.

Check the real file size. Look at the file in your file manager, not at the tool's estimate. Estimates are computed before the final encode and can be out by several kilobytes.

Open it at 100%. If the face looks blocky or soft on your own screen at full size, it will look worse printed on an admit card. That usually means you compressed too hard or started from a poor source.

Keep the files, and name them

You will need the same photograph and signature again — at the admit card stage, at document verification, and for every other exam you apply to in the same season.

Keep a folder with the prepared files named for the exam and specification, for example ibps-photo-200x230-35kb.jpg. A folder of files called IMG_20261015.jpg is how the wrong image gets uploaded under time pressure.

Where you process these files matters

A photograph of your face and an image of your signature are identity documents. Most free resizing sites upload your file to a server to do the work, which is fine for a holiday snap and not fine for a signature.

Prefer a tool that processes the image inside your browser, so the file never leaves your device, and treat any site that requires an account to resize an image as a reason to look elsewhere.
