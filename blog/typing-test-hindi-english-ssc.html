⌨️ Typing Test Practice for SSC CHSL — Hindi & English

October 2026 · 9 min read

The SSC typing test is a qualifying stage, not a scoring one — you either clear the required speed and accuracy or you do not, and no amount of margin above it helps. That changes how to prepare: the goal is reliably clearing a threshold under pressure, not a personal best.

What is actually required

Typing requirements differ by post and are stated in each notification, so confirm yours. The common shape is a passage to be typed in a fixed time, in English or Hindi, with errors counted against you.

Two things matter more than the raw speed figure:

Accuracy is weighted heavily. Errors are deducted, so typing fast and carelessly scores worse than typing moderately and cleanly. Someone at 35 wpm with 98% accuracy usually outperforms someone at 45 wpm with 90%.

Backspacing may be restricted. Some test software disables correction entirely. If yours does, practising with free backspacing trains the wrong habit — you learn to type fast and fix, and in the test the fixing is unavailable.

Hindi typing: the layout decides everything

This is the biggest single decision for Hindi typing and it is often made by accident.

Remington mimics the mechanical Hindi typewriter layout. It is what traditional typing institutes teach and what many older government tests specify.

Inscript is the government standard layout, arranged phonetically — vowels on one side, consonants grouped by articulation. It is built into Windows and most systems, and is more logical to learn from scratch.

They are completely different. Practising on one and testing on the other means starting again, so check which layout your notification specifies before a single hour of practice. People have lost months to this.

A further point specific to Hindi: the matra and conjunct characters mean a single visible syllable may take two or three keystrokes, so the words-per-minute figures are not comparable with English.

Touch typing is the only approach that gets there

Looking at the keyboard caps you at roughly 30 wpm, because your eyes have to travel between the screen, the keyboard and the passage. Touch typing removes one of those journeys.

The home position is the whole of it: left fingers on A, S, D, F and right on J, K, L and semicolon, with thumbs on the space bar. F and J usually have a raised bump so you can find the position without looking. Every key is reached from there and the finger returns.

Learning this costs about two weeks of feeling slower than before. That dip is real and it is why most people abandon it on day three — and why those who get through it end up at double the speed of those who did not.

Accuracy first, speed second

This order is not a preference; it is how the skill forms.

Typing a wrong key teaches your hand the wrong movement. Practising fast and inaccurate trains errors, and untraining them later takes longer than learning correctly would have.

The method: type slowly enough to be accurate — genuinely slowly, at first — and let speed arrive from familiarity rather than effort. Once a movement is automatic it is fast without being rushed.

A practical rule: if your accuracy in practice is below 95%, you are going too fast for your current level. Slow down until it is above 95%, then let it drift up.

What to practise on

Random word lists build key familiarity. Real passages build the thing the test measures, because they contain punctuation, capitals, numbers and the rhythm of actual sentences.

Three things worth practising specifically, because they are where speed collapses:

  • Capitals. Shift with the opposite hand, not the same one. Most people learn this wrong and it costs time on every capital.
  • Numbers and symbols. The top row is the weakest for nearly everyone, and official passages contain dates and figures.
  • Punctuation. Commas, full stops and brackets break the flow until they are automatic.

A practice schedule

  1. Twenty minutes daily, not two hours weekly. Motor skills consolidate with frequency, not duration.
  2. Five minutes of drills on your weakest keys.
  3. Ten minutes of real passages at an accuracy-first pace.
  4. Five minutes timed, at test length and under test conditions.

The last five minutes matter most as the test approaches. Typing calmly in practice and freezing in a timed window is common, and the only fix is to practise timed.

Test-day conditions you can prepare for

  • An unfamiliar keyboard. Centre keyboards are often stiff or worn. Practise occasionally on a different keyboard rather than only your own.
  • Reading from a screen, not paper. If the passage appears on screen, practise that way — the eye movement differs.
  • No backspace. If correction is disabled, practise with it disabled.
  • Sitting posture. Wrists level, feet flat, screen at eye height. Cramped posture costs speed within ten minutes.

Honest expectations

From a starting point of hunt-and-peck, reaching a reliable 35 wpm with good accuracy typically takes two to three months of daily practice. Reaching it in two weeks is not realistic, and plans that assume it produce the fast-and-inaccurate habit described above.

The progression most people see: a slow and frustrating first two weeks while touch typing becomes possible, then steady gains for a month, then a plateau that breaks only with deliberate work on the specific keys you keep missing.
