📋 Free Online Kanban Board and To-Do List

October 2026 · 9 min read

A kanban board is three columns and some cards, which makes it look like a decorated to-do list. The difference is one rule that most people who adopt the board never apply, and without it the board is indeed just a decorated list.

The rule that makes it work

Kanban's central idea is a limit on work in progress: a cap on how many cards may sit in the "doing" column at once. Not a target, a hard ceiling. If the limit is three and three cards are in progress, you may not start a fourth — you must finish or explicitly park something first.

This is uncomfortable, which is why people skip it, and it is the entire mechanism. Without a limit the middle column fills up, everything is half-done, and the board becomes a record of things you have started.

The reason it works is that switching between tasks has a real cost. Each switch requires reloading context — where you were, what you had decided, what was next — and that reload is not free. Three tasks worked sequentially finish sooner than three worked in parallel, even though parallel feels faster.

Choosing a limit

For an individual, two or three. Not one, because you need somewhere to go when a task genuinely blocks on someone else. Not five, because that is not a limit.

The test of whether your limit is right: when you hit it, do you feel mild frustration? That is the correct feeling. It is the signal that you are about to start something before finishing something, and the limit is doing its job.

Columns that reflect reality

Three columns is the starting point and often not enough, because it hides the state where work actually stalls.

To do → Doing → Done works for simple, self-contained tasks. But most stalled work is not being worked on and not finished — it is waiting. Waiting for a reply, an approval, a document, somebody else.

Adding a Waiting column changes what the board tells you. Cards there are honestly not in progress, so they do not consume your limit, and a card sitting in Waiting for two weeks is visible as a problem rather than hidden among things you are "working on".

ColumnMeansCounts against the limit?
To doDecided, not startedNo
DoingActively being worked on todayYes
WaitingBlocked on someone elseNo, but review weekly
DoneFinishedNo

Card size is the other thing people get wrong

A card reading "finish the project" will sit in Doing for a month and tell you nothing. A card reading "reply to three emails" is noise.

The useful size is one sitting — something you could plausibly finish in a session. That makes movement across the board meaningful, which is what produces the sense of progress the board is supposed to give you.

If a card has not moved in a week, it is almost always too big. Split it, and the split itself usually reveals what was actually blocking you.

Kanban versus a list, concretely

A list is better for some things and worse for others, and knowing which saves effort.

A list is better for things with no state — shopping, a packing list, steps in a fixed procedure. Adding a board's ceremony to those adds nothing.

A board is better when items have a state and the state matters: anything with a waiting phase, anything where you can only do a few at a time, anything where you want to see where work is piling up.

For exam preparation specifically, a board works well because topics genuinely have states — not started, being studied, revised once, revision due — and because the work-in-progress limit prevents the common failure of having eight subjects half-prepared.

Using it for study

A layout that fits preparation better than the generic one:

  • Not started — topics from the syllabus, one card each.
  • Studying — limit of two. This is the discipline: two topics at a time, not eight.
  • Revision due — studied once, needs a second pass. The column that matters most and the one a plain list cannot express.
  • Solid — recalled correctly twice with a gap.

The point of the third column is that it makes the thing people skip visible. A topic studied once and never revisited is not learned, and on a list it looks identical to a topic that is.

The habits that keep a board useful

Boards die in predictable ways, and each has a cheap fix.

  • The Done column grows forever. Archive it weekly. Keeping four hundred finished cards visible makes the board slow to read and hides what is current.
  • To do becomes a dumping ground. If it holds sixty cards you will stop opening the board. Keep a separate list for someday-maybe and only promote things you intend to do.
  • Cards get stale. A weekly pass asking "is this still worth doing" removes more cards than it moves, and that is the point.
  • The limit gets ignored. Once you break it twice it stops existing. Either hold it or lower it to something you will actually respect.

Where the board should live

A board's content is a detailed picture of your work or study: what you are doing, what you are stuck on, what you have abandoned. For professional work it may include client names and unreleased plans.

Most board tools store this on their servers, which is how sharing and sync work. For a team that is the point. For an individual board it is data you did not need to transmit, and one more account to maintain.

A browser-based board keeps everything on your device. You lose sync across machines; you keep the certainty that nothing was uploaded, and it works offline.
