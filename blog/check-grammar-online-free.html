October 2026 · 9 min read

Automated grammar checking is genuinely useful and genuinely limited, and knowing which is which saves a lot of wasted editing. A checker is very good at the mechanical faults a tired writer misses. It is poor at anything requiring knowledge of what you meant, and it is actively unhelpful when it flags correct Indian English as an error.

This guide covers what to fix, what to ignore, and the specific mistakes that matter in exam and professional writing.

What a checker finds reliably

These are pattern problems — detectable from the text alone, with no understanding needed:

  • Repeated words. "the the", "is is". Invisible when you reread your own writing because your eye supplies what you meant.
  • Double spaces and spacing around punctuation. A space before a full stop, or none after a comma.
  • Sentences that run too long. Not an error exactly, but a sentence past about forty words is usually two sentences fighting.
  • Passive constructions. Detectable from the auxiliary plus participle shape.
  • Overused adverbs and filler. "very", "really", "basically", "actually" — each individually fine, collectively a sign of unedited prose.
  • Repetition of the same word. Using "important" six times in a paragraph is something you cannot see while writing.

What a checker cannot judge

These require knowing your intent, and a tool that claims to fix them will sometimes make your writing worse.

  • The right word. "Affect" and "effect", "principal" and "principle" — both spellings are valid words, so only meaning distinguishes them. A checker can guess from context and will be wrong often enough to matter.
  • Whether the argument holds. A grammatically perfect paragraph can be nonsense. This is the whole of essay marking and no tool touches it.
  • Register. Whether a sentence is too casual for an official letter is a judgement about the reader, not the grammar.
  • Deliberate style. A short fragment for emphasis. Like that. A checker reads it as an error.

Indian English is not an error

This matters because most checkers are trained predominantly on American and British usage, and will flag perfectly standard Indian English as wrong.

FlaggedVerdict
Lakh, croreCorrect and standard in Indian writing. Keep them.
PreponeStandard Indian English, widely understood. Fine in Indian contexts.
"Do the needful"Understood in Indian offices, dated elsewhere. Depends on the reader.
"Revert back"Genuinely redundant — "revert" already means to go back. Worth fixing.
-ise versus -izeBoth valid. Indian usage follows British -ise. Just be consistent.

The rule: a checker does not know who is reading your document. For a UPSC answer or an Indian government application, Indian English is correct. For an international job application, the house style of the recipient is what matters.

Passive voice: a signal, not a rule

"Mistakes were made" hides who made them, and that is why it is overused in official writing. But passive voice is often the right choice.

Use the active when the actor matters: "The committee rejected the proposal." Use the passive when the actor is unknown, irrelevant, or genuinely less important than the thing acted upon: "The bridge was built in 1924." Nobody needs the contractor's name there.

Scientific writing uses the passive deliberately, because the method matters and the person performing it does not. A checker that flags every passive construction is giving you a count, not a verdict. Twenty passives in ten sentences is worth examining; three in a page is normal prose.

Sentence length and readability

Readability formulas count syllables and words per sentence and produce a grade level. Treat the number as a rough signal rather than a target.

A useful reading: if your average sentence is over about twenty-five words, the writing is probably harder than it needs to be. If every sentence is under ten, it will feel choppy. Variety is what reads well — a long sentence that develops an idea, then a short one that lands it.

For exam answers there is a practical point beyond style. An examiner reading a hundred scripts has limited patience for a forty-word sentence with three subordinate clauses. Short, direct sentences are easier to mark favourably.

The errors that actually cost marks

In exam and professional writing, these are worth a dedicated pass because a checker finds some and misses others.

  • Subject-verb agreement across a long subject. "The list of candidates who applied last year were published" — should be "was", because the subject is "the list", not "candidates". A checker catches the simple cases and misses this one.
  • Article use. Missing or extra "a", "an", "the" is the most common second-language marker and the hardest to self-check. Read aloud; the ear catches what the eye does not.
  • Tense consistency. Starting a paragraph in the past and drifting into the present.
  • Dangling modifiers. "Having studied for six months, the exam was easy" — the exam did not study. Reads fine until someone points it out.
  • Apostrophes in plurals. "The candidate's were informed" is wrong; so is "it's" for possession.
  • Comma splices. Two full sentences joined by a comma. Needs a full stop, a semicolon, or a conjunction.

A sequence that catches more than one pass

Reading the same text repeatedly stops finding errors because you begin reading what you intended. Changing the mode of reading breaks that.

  1. Write without stopping. Editing while drafting produces slow, cautious prose.
  2. Leave it. Even ten minutes helps; overnight is better.
  3. Run the checker for the mechanical faults — repeats, spacing, obvious agreement.
  4. Read aloud. This is the highest-value step and the one people skip. Missing articles, wrong tenses and sentences that run out of breath are all audible.
  5. Read backwards, last sentence first, for spelling only. Without the flow of argument, your eye sees the words themselves.
  6. Check the facts and figures separately. Grammar tools do not know that your date is wrong.

Privacy, which matters more than it seems

Grammar checking means sending your text somewhere. If that text is a cover letter, it contains your employment history. If it is an exam answer, it is your own work. If it is a legal or medical document, it is somebody else's confidential information.

Most online checkers upload the text to a server, and the better-known ones state in their terms that submitted content may be used to improve their models. For a blog draft that is unimportant. For a document you are paid to keep confidential, it is a problem that has nothing to do with grammar.

A checker that runs in your browser cannot do everything a large cloud model can. What it can do is the entire mechanical layer — repeats, spacing, passive constructions, long sentences, overused words, readability — without your text leaving the device.
