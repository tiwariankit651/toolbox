📝 Free MCQ Quiz Maker for Teachers — Create Online Tests Instantly

October 2026 · 9 min read

Setting a quiz for a class has a practical problem that has nothing to do with the questions: distribution. You can write twenty good questions in an hour, and then spend as long again getting them to thirty students, collecting answers, and marking them.

This guide covers the question-writing side properly, and then the distribution options with their actual trade-offs — including the privacy consideration that applies when the students are minors.

Writing questions that diagnose

The purpose of a class quiz is usually not to rank students. It is to find out what the class did not understand, so the next lesson can address it. That changes what a good question looks like.

A question everyone answers correctly tells you nothing you did not know. A question everyone gets wrong usually means the teaching was unclear rather than the class inattentive. The informative questions are the ones that split the room, because the split tells you which misconception is present.

To get that, the wrong options have to be the misconceptions you suspect. If you think half the class confuses mean with median, make median an option. The number who choose it is the diagnostic result.

The leaks that make a question free

Easy to introduce without noticing, and students find them quickly.

  • The longest option is right. Writers qualify the correct answer into precision and leave the distractors short.
  • Grammar that only fits one option. A stem ending in "an" eliminates every option starting with a consonant.
  • Absolutes only in wrong answers. "Always" and "never" are reliable elimination signals if you only ever use them in distractors.
  • A keyword repeated from the stem. If the stem says "photosynthesis" and one option does too, that is a hint.
  • "All of the above" with two obviously correct options. Settles itself.

A quick self-check: read the four options without the question. If you can pick the likely answer, it is leaking.

About a minute per straightforward recall question and two for anything requiring a calculation is a reasonable planning figure. Students who finish very early usually guessed; students who do not finish were set too many.

Distribution, with the real trade-offs

Printed paper. Works without devices, no privacy question at all, and marking thirty papers takes half an hour. Still the right answer for a formal assessment.

A shared link to an online quiz. Instant distribution and automatic marking. The cost is that most platforms require accounts, and if the students are minors that means collecting personal data from children, which in many institutions needs consent you do not have.

A self-contained file. Export the quiz and share the file — email, a group, a shared drive. Students open it, attempt it, and tell you the score. No accounts, no uploads, and it works on a phone without a connection after loading.

The third option loses automatic collection of results. For a diagnostic quiz, where you want the distribution of answers rather than individual scores, a show of hands after the quiz gives you that faster than a dashboard would.

The privacy point, stated plainly

If you are a teacher asking a class to use an online tool, you are directing minors to a third party. Any platform requiring a sign-up is collecting data about children on your instruction.

This is worth checking before sharing a link, not afterwards. The questions to ask: does it require an account, what does it store, and where. A tool that runs entirely in the browser and stores nothing on a server removes the question rather than answering it.

Explanations do the teaching

A quiz that reports a score has done assessment. A quiz that explains each answer has done teaching, and the second is where the value is for a class.

Write the explanation for the student who chose the most tempting wrong answer. Not "the answer is (b)" but "many people choose (c) because X, but X applies only when Y". That addresses the misconception directly, in the moment the student is most receptive — immediately after being wrong.

It also halves your follow-up work, because the common confusion is answered thirty times at once.

Reusing and adapting

A quiz written once should serve several years, which means storing it in a form you can edit.

A plain structured file — CSV or JSON — is editable in a spreadsheet, diffable, and openable in ten years. A quiz locked inside a platform's account is not, and platforms close.

Practical approach: keep the master as a file you own, import it into whatever tool you use for delivery, and edit the master. Then adapting last year's quiz is a five-minute job rather than a rewrite.

Reading the results

Three patterns and what each means:

  • One question wrong across most of the class. The teaching was unclear. Reteach that point; do not blame the students.
  • One distractor chosen by many. A specific, identifiable misconception. The most useful result a quiz can produce, and the one a score alone hides.
  • Wrong answers spread evenly across options. The class was guessing, which means the topic has not landed at all.
