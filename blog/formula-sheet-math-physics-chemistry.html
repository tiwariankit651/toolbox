📐 Formula Sheet — Quick Reference

October 2026 · 9 min read

A formula sheet is useful or useless depending on one thing: whether you built it. A downloaded sheet of four hundred formulas is a reference document. A sheet you assembled from the formulas you personally kept forgetting is a study tool, and the difference is the act of selection.

Why a comprehensive sheet does not work

Four hundred formulas on six pages cannot be revised. There is no order of importance, no indication of which ones appear in exams, and no connection to what you specifically find hard. Opening it produces a vague sense of having revised.

The sheet that works is short — one page, perhaps forty formulas — and contains only the ones you have actually got wrong. That set is different for every student, which is why no published sheet can be it.

Understand the derivation, memorise the result

These are not alternatives, and treating them as such is the most common mistake in both directions.

Memorising without understanding fails when a question is phrased unusually, because you cannot tell which formula applies. Understanding without memorising fails in a timed exam, because deriving takes minutes you do not have.

The practical approach: derive each formula once, properly, so you know where it comes from and what its assumptions are. Then memorise the result for speed. The derivation is insurance — if you blank in the exam, you can reconstruct it; and if a question tests whether you know the assumption, you do.

A concrete case: knowing that the kinematic equations assume constant acceleration is the difference between applying them correctly and applying them to a problem where acceleration varies.

Group by relationship, not by chapter

Textbook order is pedagogical, not mnemonic. Grouping by what connects formulas makes them far easier to hold.

  • By quantity. Everything that produces a force in one place; everything that produces energy in another. In an exam you know what you need to find, so that is the useful index.
  • By derivation. Formulas derived from the same starting point belong together, because remembering one lets you reconstruct the others.
  • By the mistake you make. A section for the pairs you confuse — permutations against combinations, series against parallel — with the distinguishing feature written next to them.

That last grouping is the most valuable and appears on no published sheet, because the confusions are personal.

Dimensional analysis: the check that catches real errors

Every physical formula must balance dimensionally, and checking takes seconds.

If you recall a formula as v = u + at², the check fails immediately: velocity is length per time, and at² is length per time squared times time squared, which is length. Length cannot equal length per time, so the formula is wrong — and you know this without remembering the correct version.

This is the single most useful habit in physics problem solving. It catches misremembered formulas, dropped factors with dimensions, and unit conversion errors. It does not catch a wrong dimensionless constant — it will not tell you whether the ½ in ½at² is right — but it catches the structural mistakes, which are the expensive ones.

Writing it by hand

There is a real effect here worth using. Writing a formula by hand, from memory, engages recall in a way that typing or copying does not. The act of reproducing it is itself the practice.

So: build the sheet by writing each formula from memory, then checking. The ones you cannot write are exactly the ones that belong on the sheet, and you have just identified them.

Keep the digital version for searching and the handwritten one for revising. They serve different purposes.

What belongs on a one-page sheet

Four categories, and nothing else.

  1. Formulas you have got wrong in practice. The core of the sheet, and the only ones that are definitely yours.
  2. Constants you keep looking up. g, c, R, Avogadro — with units, because a constant without units is a number.
  3. Pairs you confuse, written side by side with the difference marked.
  4. Conversions you fumble. eV to joules, atm to pascals, the ones your particular syllabus keeps using.

What does not belong: anything you have never got wrong, anything you can derive in ten seconds, and anything outside your syllabus.

The sheet should shrink

This is the measure of progress and the reason it works. A formula you have recalled correctly three times, with gaps between, comes off the sheet. One you keep failing gets a note about why you fail it — the sign, the units, the condition you forget.

A sheet that grows all term is a collection. A sheet that shrinks is evidence of learning, and in the last week before an exam a half-page of genuinely difficult formulas is worth more than six pages of everything.

Revising from it properly

Reading a formula sheet is close to useless, for the same reason reading a vocabulary list is: the answer is visible, so no recall happens.

Instead: cover the formula, read only its name or the quantity it gives, and write it from memory. Then check. That effort before looking is where the learning is.

Two or three passes a week, five minutes each, beats one long session — the spacing does more than the duration.
