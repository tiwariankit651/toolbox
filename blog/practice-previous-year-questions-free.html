📝 Practice Previous Year Questions

October 2026 · 10 min read

Previous year questions are the highest-value material in exam preparation, and most candidates use them in the least effective way: as a test at the end, taken once, scored, and set aside. Used properly they are not a test at all. They are the specification for what you need to learn, and they should be the first thing you open rather than the last.

Why they beat any other source

A question paper is the only document that tells you what the examiner actually values, as opposed to what a syllabus permits them to ask.

A syllabus for General Awareness might list "Indian Polity" — which covers several hundred pages. The previous papers tell you that the questions concentrate heavily on the Fundamental Rights, the amendment process, and the constitutional bodies, and that large areas of the same syllabus appear once in five years. That is a difference of weeks of study time.

Three things only past papers can tell you:

  • Depth. Whether a topic is asked as a one-line fact or as an application. "Which Article deals with X" and "in situation Y, which Article applies" need completely different preparation.
  • Weighting. How many marks a topic has actually carried, averaged over several years — not how much space it takes in a textbook.
  • Phrasing. The specific way an examiner words a question, which is what you will be reading under time pressure.

Start with them, do not finish with them

The usual sequence is: study everything, then attempt past papers, then discover that half of what you studied never appears and a quarter of what appears you skipped.

Reversing it costs one afternoon and saves weeks. Before studying a subject, read through two or three years of its questions — not answering, just reading. You will finish with a clear sense of what the examiner asks about, which turns the textbook from a thing to be covered into a thing to be consulted.

This also inoculates you against the most common waste in preparation: learning a topic to a depth nobody tests.

The analysis that produces a plan

Scoring a paper tells you a number. Analysing one tells you what to do, and that requires separating your wrong answers by cause.

Why it was wrongWhat it meansWhat to do
Did not know itA genuine gapStudy that topic
Knew it, recalled wronglyWeak retentionRevision schedule, not more reading
Misread the questionReading under pressurePractise timed, not untimed
Ran out of timePacing or orderChange the sequence you attempt in
Guessed and lost marksNegative marking disciplineDecide a guessing rule and keep it

These five causes call for five different responses, and treating them all as "revise more" is why people plateau. Someone whose errors are mostly category two does not need to read anything new — they need a review schedule. Someone in category four needs no extra knowledge at all.

Keep a one-line note per wrong answer with its cause. After three papers the pattern is obvious and the plan writes itself.

Repeats are real, and worth exploiting

Questions do repeat — rarely word for word, often in substance. A fact that appeared in 2019 is a fact the examiner considers important, and the same fact can return as a differently worded question.

The implication: a question you got wrong is worth more attention than its single mark suggests, because the underlying fact has a real chance of returning. A question you got right by guessing is worth flagging for the same reason.

This is also why the same paper is worth attempting twice, several weeks apart. The second attempt is not a memory test if the gap is long enough; it is a retention test, and retention is what the exam measures.

Timed versus untimed

Both have a place and they are not interchangeable.

Untimed is for learning. Take as long as you need, look things up afterwards, understand why each wrong answer was wrong. This is where the actual learning happens.

Timed is for the skill the exam tests, which is answering under pressure. Misreading a question, panicking at a hard one, and spending four minutes on something worth one mark are failures that only appear with a clock running.

A workable split is roughly three untimed sessions for every timed one early in preparation, inverting as the exam approaches. Only timed practice, from the start, means you never properly learn anything. Only untimed practice means you have never experienced the actual conditions.

Negative marking changes the arithmetic

Where wrong answers cost marks, guessing has a break-even point you can calculate.

With four options and a penalty of one-quarter of the mark for a wrong answer, a pure random guess is exactly break-even over many questions: one right at +1 and three wrong at −0.25 each sum to zero. So random guessing gains nothing and risks variance.

But eliminating even one option changes it. With three plausible options left, random choice among them is positive expected value. With two, clearly so.

The practical rule: guess when you can eliminate at least one option, skip when you cannot. Decide that before the exam and follow it, because the decision is not one you can make well in the hall.

Building a question bank from your own errors

The most valuable practice set is not a commercial one. It is the questions you personally got wrong, because those are the gaps specific to you.

After each paper, take every wrong answer and write it as a question you will face again. Not the original wording — your own, phrased to test the fact rather than the trick. Then review that set on a spaced schedule, and remove a question only after getting it right twice with a gap between.

A set of two hundred questions assembled this way is worth more than two thousand generic ones, and it shrinks as you learn, which is a visible measure of progress.

Where to find the papers

Official commission websites publish question papers and answer keys for recent years, and these are the authoritative versions. Coaching compilations are convenient but introduce transcription errors, and an answer key that is wrong is worse than no key.

If you use a compilation, spot-check a few answers against the official key. One wrong answer learned confidently costs more than several questions skipped.

A schedule that holds

  1. Before starting a subject: read two or three years of its questions without answering. One sitting.
  2. While studying: after each topic, attempt only that topic's past questions. Immediate, specific feedback.
  3. Weekly: one full paper, untimed, with analysis by error cause.
  4. From two months out: one full paper a week, timed, at the real exam's time of day.
  5. Throughout: review your own error bank on a spaced schedule.
