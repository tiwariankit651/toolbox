📊 Free Syllabus Tracker for Government Exams — UPSC, SSC, Banking

October 2026 · 9 min read

A syllabus is a list of what may be asked. It says nothing about what is asked, how deeply, or how often — and those three things decide where your time should go. Treating the list as a plan is the most common structural error in exam preparation.

Weight the syllabus before you use it

Go through two or three years of question papers and count the questions per topic. The distribution is always uneven, and usually more uneven than expected.

You will find topics appearing every year without fail, and topics that appeared once in five years. The syllabus gives both a single line. Without the count, the natural tendency is to spend equal effort on each, which is how candidates lose three weeks to something worth two marks.

Record the count next to each topic. Then a completion figure means something: 60% of the marks, not 60% of the lines.

Done and not-done is the wrong model

A checkbox cannot distinguish a topic read once in June from one revised three times in September, and those are entirely different situations.

The transition that decides outcomes is from "read once" to anything beyond it. A topic read once is not learned, and most candidates' syllabi are largely in that state a month before the exam — which is why the final weeks feel like starting again.

Granularity: one sitting per entry

"Indian Polity" is not trackable. It will sit in progress for a month and tell you nothing.

"Fundamental Rights, Articles 12 to 18" is one sitting. It changes state, which makes progress visible, and visible progress is what keeps a tracker in use past the second week.

A reliable signal: if an entry has not changed state in two weeks, it is too large. Split it, and the split usually reveals which part was actually blocking you.

Track by subject, not by exam

Most aspirants prepare for several exams whose syllabi overlap heavily — General Awareness, Reasoning and Quantitative Aptitude appear in nearly all of them.

Keeping a separate tracker per exam duplicates everything and means updating the same topic three times. One entry per subject, noting which exams need it, is less work and gives a truer picture. Where an exam needs a topic at a different depth, note that against the exam rather than duplicating the topic.

Revision has to be dated, not deferred

Revision appears in every plan and is the first thing dropped, because it produces no new coverage and feels like standing still.

The fix is to give it a date. A topic read on the 3rd is due on the 10th, and that due date sits in the tracker as a scheduled item. Overdue revision then shows up as overdue rather than disappearing into a vague intention.

Spacing intervals that work: same day, next day, three days, a week, a month. A topic still correct after the month will hold until the exam.

Percentage complete, honestly

A tracker reporting 70% is usually overstating, for two reasons: it counts a one-mark topic equally with a twenty-mark one, and it counts "read once" as complete.

A truer figure weights by both marks and state — perhaps a third of the weight for read-once, two-thirds for revised, full for solid. The number comes out lower and moves only when you actually learn something, which is the behaviour you want from a progress measure.

Planning arithmetic that survives

Dividing topics by days available gives a schedule that breaks in week two and is then abandoned.

Plan new material for about five days a week. The remaining two absorb overruns and revision. If nothing overran you are ahead — which rarely happens, and the slack is why the plan is still alive in month three.

What to review weekly

  1. Overdue revisions. Clear these before adding new topics.
  2. Anything stuck for two weeks. Too big, or being avoided. Both need a decision.
  3. Marks-weighted progress by subject. One subject far behind is the thing to correct.
  4. High-weight topics not yet solid. These are where marks are available.

Fifteen minutes once a week. Without it a tracker becomes a record of the past rather than a plan.

Where the data should live

A tracker needs to work when the connection does not, and to still exist in eight months. A tool requiring an account can lock you out at the wrong moment; one storing locally cannot sync across devices.

Whichever you choose, the non-negotiable is an export. A year of tracking inside a service with no way to extract it is data you do not own.
