🏦 IBPS PO Photo & Signature Size Requirements 2026

October 2026 · 10 min read

IBPS publishes its image specifications in an instruction page attached to every notification, and the numbers have been stable for several cycles. The photograph should be 200 × 230 pixels and between 20 and 50 KB; the signature should be between 10 and 20 KB; both in JPEG. The instruction also states plainly that the scanned image must not exceed 50 KB — which is why a file that looks fine on your phone gets refused at the upload step.

Those four numbers are easy to find. What causes rejections is everything around them, so this guide covers the parts the specification does not spell out.

The numbers, and where they come from

Several recent IBPS recruitments also require a live photograph captured during the application in addition to the uploaded one, and some require a handwritten declaration. Which of these applies depends on the specific recruitment, so read the instruction page attached to your notification rather than assuming the previous cycle's flow.

Target the middle of the band, not the edge

"20 to 50 KB" is inclusive at both ends and enforced. Compressing to 19 KB fails just as surely as 51 KB, and the portal's error message rarely says which.

There is a second reason to aim for the middle. Some portals re-encode what you upload, and a re-encode can shift the file size by a few kilobytes in either direction. A file at 48 KB can come out the other side above the ceiling. A file at 35 KB has room.

Why 200 × 230 and not a passport ratio

200 ÷ 230 is 0.87. A printed passport photograph at 3.5 × 4.5 cm is 0.78. They are not the same shape, so a scan of a printed passport photo forced into 200 × 230 is slightly stretched horizontally.

The fix is to crop rather than squash. Take your source photograph, crop a region whose width is about 87% of its height — face centred, a small margin above the head, shoulders in frame — and only then resize that crop to 200 × 230. Done in that order there is no distortion at all.

If your tool offers "fit" and "fill", fill is what you want for this, with the crop centred on the face. Fit will add white bars to preserve the original ratio, and bars are not acceptable.

The signature: running hand, not block capitals

IBPS instructions state that the candidate's signature should be in running handwriting. A name printed in capital letters is not treated as a signature, and this is checked.

It also has to match what you will write on the attendance sheet and in the examination hall. Candidates sometimes produce a careful, decorative signature for the form and then sign normally on the day; a mismatch at that point is a far more serious problem than a rejected upload, because it is raised in front of you with no chance to correct it.

Practical approach: on plain white paper, draw a light rectangle about 6 cm wide and 2 cm tall, sign inside it the way you always sign, and crop to that rectangle. A signature floating in a large white field either scans too large or, once cropped tightly, ends up with no margin at all.

Scanning versus photographing the signature

A flatbed scanner at 200 DPI in greyscale gives the cleanest result and the smallest file. If you do not have one, a phone photograph works provided you handle two things.

Shadow. Holding the phone directly over the paper puts your own shadow across it. Light the paper from the side, or photograph near a window with the paper angled towards it.

Perspective. A photograph taken at an angle makes the rectangle a trapezium, and the signature looks tilted when cropped square. Hold the phone parallel to the paper.

Then convert to greyscale before compressing. A colour photograph of black ink carries three channels of data describing something that is essentially one channel, and removing the other two shrinks the file dramatically with no visible loss.

Why signatures compress badly and photos do not

JPEG assumes photographic content: gradual changes in colour across an area. A signature is hard black edges on flat white — exactly what JPEG handles worst. Compress it to 10 KB and you get grey fringes around every stroke, with thin strokes breaking into dotted lines.

A face, by contrast, compresses gracefully. 30 KB is plenty for a clear 200 × 230 portrait.

So treat them differently: push the photograph to the middle of its band without worrying, and keep the signature towards the upper end of its own band so the strokes stay solid.

What to prepare before you open the form

  1. Photograph at 200 × 230 px, 30–40 KB, JPEG, from the best original you have.
  2. Signature at 14–18 KB, JPEG, greyscale, running hand.
  3. A spare of each at slightly different compression, in case the portal rejects one without saying why.
  4. Good light where you are sitting, if the recruitment includes a live photo capture.
  5. A handwritten declaration if your notification asks for one — usually a specified sentence in your own hand, photographed or scanned to the same standard as the signature.

Name the files with your registration number once you have it. Over a banking season you will apply to several recruitments and need these again at the admit card and document verification stages, and a folder of files called IMG_20261015.jpg is how the wrong photograph gets uploaded.

Mistakes that cause real rejections

  • Starting from a WhatsApp image. WhatsApp recompresses on send, so you are compressing an already-compressed file. At 30 KB the blocking is visible. Move the original across by cable, email or a file-sharing link instead.
  • Screenshotting a photograph. The result is the resolution of your screen, not the photograph, and it looks soft at any size.
  • Compressing before resizing. Compression artefacts then get scaled along with the image. Resize first, compress last.
  • A busy background. A plain light wall is specified for a reason — the photograph is used for identification.
  • Glasses with glare. If the lens reflection hides an eye, the photograph can be refused at verification even though it uploaded.
  • Leaving it to the closing day. Portals slow down near the deadline, and a failed upload with an hour remaining is how an application is lost.

A note on where you process these files

An image of your signature is a genuine identity document. Many free resizing sites upload your file to a server, process it there and send it back, and their retention policy is usually a paragraph nobody reads.

For a holiday snap that is unimportant. For your signature it is worth avoiding entirely. Use something that works inside your browser, where the file is never transmitted, and be sceptical of any site that wants an account before it will resize an image.
