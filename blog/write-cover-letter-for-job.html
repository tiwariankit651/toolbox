📄 How to Write a Cover Letter

October 2026 · 9 min read

Most cover letters fail in the first line, and they fail the same way: by telling the reader something they already know. "I am writing to apply for the position of Junior Analyst advertised on your website" uses a sentence to convey no information, and it is the sentence the reader uses to decide whether to keep reading.

What a cover letter is for

A CV lists what you have done. A cover letter answers the question the CV cannot: why this role, and why you. If a letter could be sent to any employer with the name changed, it is not doing that job and the reader can tell immediately.

It also provides the only place to address something a CV cannot explain — a career change, a gap, a move to a new city, a qualification that does not match the listing.

The structure that works

Four paragraphs, about 250 to 350 words total. Longer is not read.

  1. Why you are writing and why them, specifically. One or two sentences.
  2. Your most relevant evidence. One or two achievements with a number, chosen because they match the listing.
  3. Why this company. Something concrete about them, not a compliment.
  4. The close. What you would like to happen next, in one line.

The opening line, concretely

Three versions of the same application:

Weak: "I am writing to apply for the Data Analyst position advertised on your careers page."
The reader knows. You have spent the most valuable sentence on nothing.

Better: "I have spent two years building reporting pipelines for a 40-person sales team, which is the problem your Data Analyst listing describes."
Connects your experience to their stated need in one sentence.

Also good: "Your listing mentions moving reporting off spreadsheets — I did exactly that at my last job, cutting a weekly four-hour report to twenty minutes."
Quotes their problem, answers it with a specific outcome.

The pattern in both good versions: their need first, your evidence second. Not your history first.

Evidence with numbers

"Improved the reporting process" is an assertion. "Cut the weekly report from four hours to twenty minutes" is evidence, and it is memorable in a way the assertion is not.

Numbers you probably have even if you have not thought of them as achievements: how many people you handled, how much time something took before and after, how many items you processed, what percentage you improved, how large a budget or a dataset was.

If genuinely no number exists, describe the situation and the outcome concretely. "Took over a backlog of 200 unresolved tickets and cleared it in six weeks" works without a percentage.

"Why this company" without flattery

This paragraph is where most letters become interchangeable, because they say something that could be said about anyone. "I admire your commitment to innovation" is not a reason.

A real reason is specific and shows you looked: a product you have used, a market they are entering, something in the job description that is unusual, a problem their industry has that you have worked on.

Two sentences is enough. The purpose is to show the application was written for them, not to praise them.

Addressing the awkward things

A cover letter is the right place for these, briefly and without apology.

  • A gap. State it plainly with what you did: "I took eight months out for a family matter and used the time to complete a data analysis certification." One sentence. No apology.
  • A career change. Name the transferable part. A teacher moving into training has directly relevant experience, and saying so is more effective than hoping it is inferred.
  • Relocating. Say you are moving and when. Employers assume a distant address means a problem unless told otherwise.
  • A missing qualification. If you meet most requirements, address the gap with what substitutes for it. Ignoring it does not make it unnoticed.

What to leave out

  • Your full history. That is the CV. The letter picks two things.
  • Salary expectations, unless asked.
  • "I am a hard-working team player with excellent communication skills." Everyone writes this, so it distinguishes nobody. Show it instead.
  • Criticism of a current employer. Always reads badly, regardless of accuracy.
  • Over-formality. "I would be most grateful for the esteemed opportunity" reads as dated. Write as you would speak to a senior colleague.

ATS and formatting

Many applications pass through software that parses the text before a person reads it, and a few formatting choices decide whether it parses correctly.

  • Plain structure. No columns, no text boxes, no header-footer content. Parsers read these out of order or not at all.
  • A standard font at 10 to 12 point.
  • PDF unless told otherwise, because it renders identically everywhere. If the system asks for .doc, follow that.
  • Use the listing's own words for skills where they are accurate. If they say "stakeholder reporting", use that phrase rather than a synonym — both the software and the reader are matching against it.

One caution: match the words honestly. Keyword-stuffing a letter with terms you cannot support fails at interview, which is a worse outcome than not being shortlisted.

Before sending

  1. Check the company name appears and is correct. A letter addressed to the wrong company ends the application.
  2. Read the first sentence alone. Does it say anything they did not know?
  3. Find the two pieces of evidence. If there are none, the letter is assertions.
  4. Count the words. Over 400 and it will be skimmed.
  5. Read aloud. Catches the over-formal sentences you cannot see.
  6. Name the file properly — Ankit-Tiwari-Cover-Letter-Analyst.pdf.
