📋 Track Your Exam Syllabus Progress

October 2026 · 9 min read

Most syllabus tracking fails in the same way: a list of topics with checkboxes, ticked when a topic is "done". Six weeks later the ticks are meaningless, because a topic read once in June is not in the same state as one revised three times in September, and the list cannot tell them apart.

A topic has more than two states

Done and not-done is the wrong model for learning. A useful tracker distinguishes at least four states, because each one calls for a different action.

StateMeansNext action
Not startedNever openedRead it
Read onceCovered, not retainedRevise within a week
RevisedSecond pass doneTest with questions
TestedAttempted questions, found gapsFix the specific gaps
SolidAnswered correctly twice, with a gapLight review only

The jump that matters is from "read once" to anything further, because a topic read once and never revisited is not learned — and on a checkbox list it looks exactly like one that is.

Most people's syllabus is largely "read once" six weeks before the exam, which is why the final weeks feel like starting again.

Weight by what the exam asks, not by chapter length

A syllabus lists topics without indicating which carry marks. Previous papers do, and the difference is substantial.

Before planning, go through two or three years of papers and count the questions per topic. You will find topics that appear every year and topics that appeared once in five. Treating them equally is how candidates spend three weeks on something worth two marks.

Record that count next to each topic in your tracker. Then "60% complete" means something useful — 60% of the marks, not 60% of the topics.

Percentage complete is a misleading number

A tracker showing 70% complete feels encouraging and usually is not, for two reasons.

First, unweighted completion counts a one-mark topic the same as a twenty-mark one. Second, it counts "read once" as complete, which it is not.

A more honest figure is marks-weighted and state-weighted: a topic at "read once" counts for perhaps a third of its weight, "revised" two-thirds, "solid" full. That number is lower and more useful, because it moves when you actually learn something rather than when you turn a page.

Revision is the part that gets dropped

Every plan includes revision and almost none survives contact with the calendar, because revision is invisible work — it produces no new coverage and feels like standing still.

Two things help:

Schedule revision as a topic, not as a phase. "Revise Polity" on a specific day, in the plan, with the same status as "study Economy". A revision phase at the end always gets compressed by whatever overran.

Make the revision due date visible. A topic read on the 3rd needs revisiting around the 10th. If the tracker shows that date, the overdue items are obvious. If it only shows a tick, they are not.

Realistic planning arithmetic

The commonest planning error is dividing topics by days available and treating the result as a schedule.

Two hundred topics in a hundred days is two a day, which ignores revision, ignores that topics differ in size, and ignores that some days will be lost. A plan with no slack fails in week two and then gets abandoned entirely.

A workable approach: plan to cover new material on about five days a week, not seven. The other two absorb overruns and revision. If nothing overran, you are ahead — which almost never happens, and that is the point.

Breaking topics down to a session

"Indian Polity" is not a trackable item. It will sit at "in progress" for a month and tell you nothing.

The useful granularity is one sitting — "Fundamental Rights, Articles 12 to 18" rather than "Polity". That makes the state changes meaningful and gives you the visible progress that keeps a tracker in use.

If a topic has not changed state in two weeks, it is almost always too big, and splitting it usually reveals which part was actually blocking you.

Tracking across several exams

Most aspirants prepare for more than one exam, and the syllabi overlap heavily — General Awareness, Reasoning and Quantitative Aptitude appear in most of them.

Tracking each exam separately duplicates everything. Tracking by subject and noting which exams need it is better: one entry for "Percentages", marked as relevant to three exams, in one state.

Where an exam needs a topic to a different depth, note that against the exam rather than duplicating the topic.

Where the data should live

A syllabus tracker records what you have studied, what you keep failing, and how your preparation is actually going. It is a reasonably personal document.

More practically, it needs to work when the connection does not, and it needs to still exist in eight months. A tool requiring an account is one that can lock you out at the wrong moment; a tool that stores locally cannot sync across devices.

Whichever you choose, the thing that matters is an export. A year of tracking data inside a service with no way to get it out is data you do not own.
