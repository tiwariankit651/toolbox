✏️ Convert Photo to Pencil Sketch Online

October 2026 · 8 min read

The pencil sketch effect is one of the few image filters built on a technique photographers used in a darkroom, and knowing how it works explains why some photographs convert beautifully and others turn into grey mush.

How the effect is produced

The classic method is three steps, and each one is doing something specific.

  1. Convert to greyscale. A pencil drawing has no colour, and removing it first means the later steps work on brightness alone.
  2. Blur a copy and invert it. This produces a negative of the image's broad tones, with the fine detail smoothed away.
  3. Blend the greyscale with the inverted blur using colour dodge. Where the two agree — flat areas — the result goes white. Where they disagree — edges — a dark line survives.

That last step is the whole trick. Colour dodge brightens the base according to the blend layer, and because the blend layer is an inverted blur, flat regions cancel to white and only the places where brightness changes rapidly leave a mark. Those places are edges, and edges drawn in grey on white look like pencil.

Which photographs convert well

Since the effect is driven entirely by edges, the answer follows from that.

Converts well:

  • Clear outlines against a plain background. A portrait against a wall, a building against sky.
  • Strong, directional light. Side lighting creates the brightness changes the effect needs.
  • Visible structure. Architecture, machinery, a face with defined features.

Converts badly:

  • Flat, even lighting. Overcast portraits have few hard edges, so most of the image cancels to white and you get a faint outline floating in nothing.
  • Busy backgrounds. Foliage produces thousands of small edges, and the subject disappears into noise.
  • Low-contrast scenes. Fog, a white object on a white table — nothing for the blend to find.
  • Heavily compressed images. JPEG blocking is a brightness change, so the effect draws the compression artefacts as though they were lines.

That last point is worth remembering: the sketch effect is an edge detector, and it does not know which edges you meant.

The blur radius is the main control

If a tool gives you one slider, this is usually it, and its behaviour is predictable once you know what it does.

A small radius keeps fine detail, so you get many thin lines — closer to a technical drawing. Too small and the effect barely registers, because the blur is not different enough from the original for the dodge to produce anything.

A large radius smooths away detail, leaving only the strongest edges — closer to a charcoal sketch. Too large and the image reduces to a few heavy outlines.

Portraits generally want a moderate radius: enough to lose skin texture, not so much that the eyes and mouth lose definition. Architecture can take a smaller radius because the edges are genuinely hard.

Preparing the photograph first

Three adjustments before converting improve the result more than any slider afterwards.

  • Increase contrast. The effect needs brightness differences, so giving it more to work with produces stronger lines.
  • Crop in. A tight crop on the subject removes background edges that would otherwise compete.
  • Start from a good original. A compressed file gives you its artefacts drawn as lines.

What this effect is and is not for

It is a stylisation, not a drawing. It will not turn a photograph into something that looks hand-made, because a human sketch involves choices — which lines to include, what to exaggerate, what to leave out — and an edge detector makes none of them.

Where it genuinely works: a profile picture with a distinct look, a gift print from a clear portrait, a stylised illustration for a document, a quick way to make a photograph less literal. Where it does not: passing off as an artwork, or rescuing a photograph that was not good to begin with.

Privacy, because these are usually personal photographs

The images people convert are family portraits, friends, children. Many free filter sites upload the photograph to a server to process it, and the retention terms are usually a paragraph nobody reads.

Photographs also carry EXIF metadata, which can include GPS coordinates of where it was taken and the device that took it. Uploading a family photo to apply a filter transfers all of that along with the pixels.

A browser-based filter reads the file locally and never transmits it. That is also why it works without a connection.
