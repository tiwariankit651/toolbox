💹 SIP Calculator Guide

October 2026 · 10 min read

A SIP calculator answers one question — if I invest this much monthly at this assumed return, what will I have — and the answer it gives is routinely misunderstood in two ways. People treat the assumed return as a prediction, and they ignore what inflation does to the final figure. Both mistakes make the projection look better than reality.

The arithmetic, so the output is not a black box

A monthly SIP is a series of equal investments, each compounding for a different length of time. The first instalment compounds for the whole period; the last for one month.

The standard formula is the future value of an annuity:

FV = M × [((1 + r)n − 1) ÷ r] × (1 + r)

where M is the monthly amount, r is the monthly rate (annual ÷ 12 ÷ 100) and n is the number of months. The trailing (1 + r) is there because an instalment paid at the start of each month gets one extra month of growth — an annuity due rather than an ordinary annuity.

Worked: ₹10,000 a month for 15 years at an assumed 12% annual. r = 0.01, n = 180. That gives roughly ₹50.5 lakh, against ₹18 lakh invested. The ₹32.5 lakh difference is the compounding.

Where the growth actually comes from

The shape of SIP growth surprises people, and it is worth seeing rather than being told.

Figures rounded, ₹10,000 a month, 12% assumed. Two things stand out.

The gain overtakes the amount invested somewhere around year twelve to thirteen. Before that you are mostly looking at your own money; after it, mostly at returns.

And the last five years of the twenty add about ₹50 lakh, while the first five add ₹2 lakh. That is why starting earlier matters far more than investing more — the final years are doing the heavy lifting, and you only get them by having started.

The assumed return is an assumption

This is the most important caveat and the one calculators present least clearly. The 12% in the box is not a forecast. It is a figure you chose, and the output is arithmetic on your choice.

Equity mutual fund returns are not smooth. A fund averaging 12% over fifteen years may have delivered −20% in one year and +35% in another. The calculator's smooth curve does not exist in any real portfolio, and the order of returns matters for anyone withdrawing along the way.

Useful practice: run the calculation at three rates — a pessimistic 8%, a moderate 10–11%, an optimistic 13–14% — and treat the spread as the honest answer. A single number from a single assumption gives false precision.

Inflation, which the headline figure ignores

₹50 lakh in fifteen years is not ₹50 lakh of today's purchasing power. At 6% inflation, prices roughly double every twelve years, so that ₹50 lakh buys about what ₹21 lakh buys today.

This is not a reason to avoid investing — it is a reason to use the real return rather than the nominal one when planning against a goal. If the fund returns 12% and inflation is 6%, your purchasing power grows at roughly 6% a year, not 12%.

For a goal with a known future cost — a child's education, a house deposit — inflate the goal as well. A course costing ₹10 lakh today will cost considerably more in fifteen years, and planning against today's price undershoots.

Step-up SIP, and why it changes the result so much

A step-up SIP increases the monthly amount each year, usually in line with expected salary growth.

₹10,000 a month flat for fifteen years at 12% gives about ₹50.5 lakh. The same start with a 10% annual increase gives roughly ₹87 lakh — because the larger later instalments still get years of compounding, and because your income is growing anyway.

The practical appeal is that it does not feel harder. A 10% increase on a salary that also rose 10% is the same share of income as before.

What a calculator cannot include

Four things that reduce the real outcome and appear in no SIP calculator.

  • Expense ratio. The fund's annual charge is deducted before the return you see. A 1% expense ratio on a 12% gross is 11% net, and over twenty years that difference is large.
  • Exit load. Many funds charge a percentage for redemption within a year.
  • Capital gains tax. Equity gains above the exempt threshold are taxed, and the rate depends on the holding period. The calculator shows a pre-tax figure.
  • Missed instalments. The projection assumes every instalment on time for the full period. Real SIPs get paused.

None of these invalidate the exercise. They mean the projected figure is an upper bound on a smooth path, not an expectation.

SIP is a schedule, not an asset class

A frequent confusion: SIP is not a type of investment. It is a way of investing — fixed amount, fixed interval — and you can run a SIP into an equity fund, a debt fund or gold, with completely different risk.

The benefit usually cited is rupee cost averaging: a fixed amount buys more units when prices are low and fewer when high, so the average cost per unit is lower than the average price. That is real and it is modest. The larger benefit is behavioural — an automatic instalment removes the decision of when to invest, and most people time that decision badly.

Using the output sensibly

  1. Run three rates, not one, and look at the range.
  2. Subtract the expense ratio from your assumed return before calculating.
  3. Inflate the goal if the goal has a future cost.
  4. Note the crossover year where returns overtake contributions — it tells you how long this needs to run to work.
  5. Check the step-up version. Usually the single biggest improvement available.
