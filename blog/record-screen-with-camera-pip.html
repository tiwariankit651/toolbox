🎥 Record Screen with Camera Overlay (PIP)

October 2026 · 8 min read

Screen recording with a camera inset is the standard format for a tutorial, a lecture or a bug report, and the things that make one watchable are mostly decided before you press record. The common failures are predictable: unreadable text, no audio, a file too large to send, or a recording that captured a notification you did not want shared.

Resolution and readability

The most common problem is text that cannot be read, and it is caused by recording a large screen and viewing it small.

A 2560-pixel-wide display recorded at full resolution and watched in a 640-pixel window has reduced every character to a quarter of its size. Code and terminal output become illegible.

Two fixes, and the second is better:

  • Record a region rather than the whole screen, so the content fills the frame.
  • Increase the font size before recording. Zoom the browser to 125% or 150%, raise the editor's font. This looks oversized to you and correct to the viewer, which is the trade you want.

The check: shrink your own preview to the size people will watch at. If you cannot read it, neither can they.

Audio is where recordings are lost entirely

A recording with unusable audio is unusable, and the failure is often discovered after the session cannot be repeated.

Two separate audio sources exist and they are controlled independently: the microphone, and the system or tab audio. A tutorial usually wants the microphone. A demonstration of something that makes sound needs both, and getting both requires explicitly enabling each.

Browser-based recording has a specific limitation worth knowing: capturing system audio depends on the browser and the operating system, and on some combinations it is not available at all. Test it before recording something you cannot redo.

Always record a ten-second test and play it back. Checking levels on a meter is not the same as hearing the result — a microphone can show activity and still be recording the wrong device.

The camera inset, and where to put it

A face in the corner holds attention better than a disembodied voice, which is why the format exists. Two practical points.

Size it small. It is a presence cue, not the content. Around a sixth of the frame width is plenty; larger than that competes with what you are demonstrating.

Bottom-right is the default and not always right. That corner frequently holds the thing you are pointing at — a submit button, a status bar, a terminal prompt. Check what is underneath before choosing, and move it if needed.

For lighting, face a window. The usual mistake is sitting with a window behind you, which makes you a silhouette.

Before you press record

This list exists because every item on it has ruined a recording.

  1. Turn off notifications. A message preview appearing mid-recording may contain somebody's private content, and you will have to re-record.
  2. Close other tabs, both for privacy — tab titles are readable — and to avoid clicking the wrong one.
  3. Use a clean browser profile or window. Bookmarks bars show a lot about you.
  4. Prepare the starting state. Have the page already loaded, the file already open. Watching someone navigate to the starting point is dead time.
  5. Decide the sequence. Not a script, but the three or four things you will show, in order. Unplanned recordings are long and repetitive.
  6. Test record ten seconds and check video, microphone and any system audio.

File size, and why it is often huge

Screen recordings are large because screen content changes in ways video compression handles poorly — sharp text edges everywhere, and sudden full-frame changes when you switch windows.

What actually reduces the size:

  • Record a region, not the whole screen. The biggest single factor, and it improves readability at the same time.
  • Lower the frame rate. A tutorial does not need 60fps. At 15–24fps a screen recording looks fine and the file is much smaller. Only keep a high frame rate if you are demonstrating motion.
  • Keep it short. Three five-minute videos are more useful than one fifteen-minute video, and each is easier to send.
  • Avoid unnecessary scrolling. Continuous scroll changes every pixel every frame, which is the worst case for compression.

Format, and converting afterwards

Browser recording typically produces WebM, which is efficient and well supported on the web. Some platforms and older software expect MP4.

WebM plays in every current browser and in most modern players. If a recipient needs MP4, convert — but note that converting re-encodes, so quality drops slightly. Record at a slightly higher quality than you need if you know a conversion is coming.

For a short demonstration with no audio, a GIF is sometimes better: it plays inline in a bug tracker or a chat with no player needed. Keep it under about ten seconds, because GIF is a very inefficient format and a long one is enormous.

Where the recording goes

A screen recording is an unusually revealing file. It can contain email subject lines, file names, a client's data, parts of a codebase, or a window you forgot was open.

Most online recorders upload to a server, which is how they offer a shareable link. For a public tutorial that is the point. For a bug report containing a client's screen, it is an upload you may not be permitted to make.

A browser-based recorder that writes the file locally never transmits anything, and you decide where it goes afterwards.
