🔍 How to Upscale and Enhance Images

October 2026 · 9 min read

Enlarging an image is not the reverse of shrinking it, and understanding why explains both what upscaling can do and the limit it cannot cross. Shrinking discards information you had. Enlarging has to produce information you never had, and every method of doing that is a guess — a well-informed guess, but a guess.

What the pixels actually are

A 400 × 300 image contains 120,000 colour samples. Asked for 1600 × 1200, you need 1,920,000 — sixteen times as many. Fifteen out of every sixteen pixels in the output did not exist in the input and have to come from somewhere.

That is the whole of the problem. No algorithm can recover detail that was never captured; it can only make a plausible extrapolation from the neighbours. Where the guess is easy — a smooth gradient, a flat colour — the result looks fine. Where it is hard — fine text, a repeating fabric pattern, individual hairs — it does not.

The interpolation methods, and how they differ

MethodHow it worksBest for
Nearest neighbourCopies the closest pixelPixel art, where blocky is correct
BilinearAverages the four surrounding pixelsFast, slightly soft
BicubicFits a curve through sixteen neighboursPhotographs; the usual default
LanczosWeighted sinc over a wider areaSharpest of the classical methods

Bicubic is what most tools use because it is the best general compromise. Nearest neighbour is worth knowing about for one specific case: enlarging pixel art or a QR code, where smoothing destroys exactly the hard edges that carry the meaning.

Why AI upscaling looks different

Classical interpolation only knows the pixels around each gap. A trained model has seen millions of images and knows what a face, a brick wall or a letter of text usually looks like at higher resolution, so it can produce plausible detail rather than a smooth average.

That is a genuine improvement for photographs, and it carries a specific risk worth stating: the detail is invented, not recovered. An upscaled face may gain a sharpness it never had, with features subtly different from the original. For a holiday photo this does not matter. For anything used to identify a person, or as evidence, it matters a great deal — the output is a plausible reconstruction, not a better photograph of the same thing.

Where upscaling works and where it does not

Works reasonably:

  • Modest enlargement, up to about 2×, of a reasonably sharp original.
  • Images with large smooth areas — a portrait against a plain background, a product shot.
  • Making a small logo or icon usable at a slightly larger size.

Does not work:

  • Reading text that is already illegible. If you cannot read it, the upscaler cannot either; it will produce crisp letter-shaped marks that may be the wrong letters.
  • Recovering a heavily compressed image. The blocks are now part of the data, so upscaling enlarges the blocks.
  • A screenshot of a photograph. It has the screen's resolution, and no detail to extend.
  • Out-of-focus images. Blur is lost information, not low resolution. Upscaling gives you a larger blur.
  • Very large factors. Beyond about 4× the output is mostly invention regardless of method.

Do the other repairs first

Upscaling a flawed image enlarges the flaws. The sequence that produces the best result:

  1. Start from the largest original you have. Not the WhatsApp copy — that was recompressed on send, and you would be enlarging its artefacts.
  2. Crop to what you need before enlarging, so you are not spending the upscale budget on parts you will discard.
  3. Fix exposure and colour while the file is small and fast to work with.
  4. Upscale to the target.
  5. Sharpen last, gently. Sharpening before upscaling enlarges the halos it creates.

The honest alternative: do not enlarge

Often the right answer is to avoid the enlargement entirely, and it is worth checking before reaching for a tool.

  • Ask for the original. The person who sent you a 50 KB image usually still has the 3 MB one. This is the single most effective fix and the one people skip.
  • Reshoot if you can. Thirty seconds with a phone beats any upscaler.
  • Use it smaller. A 400 px image displayed at 400 px is sharp. The same image stretched to 1200 is soft at any quality setting.
  • Change the layout. A design that needs a 2000 px hero image can often use a 600 px one differently.

Resolution, DPI and print

DPI is a printing instruction, not a property of a file. A 1200 × 1800 image is 1200 × 1800 pixels whatever its metadata says; the DPI figure only tells a printer how large to make each pixel.

So "increase the DPI to 300" does nothing on its own — it changes the intended print size, not the detail. For print, what matters is pixels divided by inches: a 4 × 6 inch print at 300 DPI needs 1200 × 1800 pixels. If you have 600 × 900, you can print it at 2 × 3 inches at 300 DPI, or at 4 × 6 at 150 DPI and accept the softness.

Where the file goes

Upscaling is computationally heavy, which is why many services do it on a server. That means uploading the image, and for a photograph of a person — a document scan, a child, an identity photo — it is worth knowing where it went and for how long.
