📸 SSC Photo and Signature Upload Guide — Sizes, Rules and Why Forms Get Rejected

October 2026 · 11 min read

Form rejection over a photograph is the most avoidable loss in the whole application process, and it still happens to thousands of candidates every cycle. The specification is published, the tools are free, and the failure is almost always one of four things: the file is a few kilobytes outside the band, the aspect ratio is wrong so the face is squashed, the signature is in capital letters, or the candidate resized a photograph that was never acceptable to begin with.

One change worth knowing first. For SSC CGL in the current cycle, the photograph is no longer uploaded at all — it is captured live through your webcam or phone camera during the application, with the date visible. Older guides describing a photo upload for CGL are out of date. The signature is still a scanned image you prepare and upload, and other SSC posts and other exam bodies continue to require an uploaded photograph. Always open the official notice for the specific post you are applying to before you prepare anything.

What the specification actually says

Figures below are from the issuing bodies' own instruction pages. They change between cycles, so treat this as the shape of the requirement and confirm the numbers in your notice.

ItemTypical requirementNotes
Photograph (IBPS)200 × 230 px preferred, 20–50 KB, JPEGMust not exceed 50 KB
Signature (IBPS, SSC)10–20 KB, JPEGRoughly 6 cm × 2 cm scanned
SSC CGL photographCaptured live in the formNo upload; have good light ready
Scan resolution200 DPI where statedHigher DPI makes the file too large

The band is a hard limit, not a guideline

A 20–50 KB requirement means exactly that. A 19 KB file is rejected and so is a 51 KB file, and the portal will usually not tell you which end you failed. This catches people who compress aggressively "to be safe" and end up under the floor.

Aim for the middle. For a 20–50 KB band, target 30–40 KB: far enough from both edges that a re-save by the portal cannot push you out, and high enough that the face is still clear.

Aspect ratio is where faces get distorted

200 × 230 pixels is not a square and not the 3.5 × 4.5 cm ratio of a printed passport photo. If you take a square crop and force it to 200 × 230, the face stretches vertically. Nobody checks this at upload, but it is visible on the admit card and in the exam hall.

The correct order of operations is: crop to the target ratio first, then resize, then compress. Cropping after resizing throws away the resolution you just preserved, and compressing before resizing wastes the compression.

200 ÷ 230 is about 0.87. So crop your source to roughly that proportion — slightly taller than wide — centred on the face, with the head occupying most of the frame and a little space above the hair.

What the photograph itself must show

Specifications vary in wording but converge on the same thing, because the photograph has to be matchable against you at the exam centre.

  • Recent. Most notices say within the last three months. A photograph where you look visibly different is a problem at verification, not at upload.
  • Plain light background. White or very light. A patterned wall or a doorway behind you makes automated checks fail.
  • Face forward, eyes open, neutral expression. No side angle, no smiling with teeth in some specifications.
  • No cap, no sunglasses. Prescription glasses are generally allowed if there is no glare hiding the eyes. Tinted lenses are not.
  • Even light, no shadow across the face. This is the most common quality failure and the easiest to fix — face a window, do not stand with your back to it.
  • Full head and upper shoulders. A tight crop that cuts the chin or the top of the head gets rejected.

A photograph taken against a bedsheet in good daylight, held at arm's length by someone else, meets all of this. A studio is not required — but a selfie taken at arm's length by yourself usually fails on angle, and a group photograph cropped down almost always fails on resolution.

The signature rules people get wrong

The signature is the part candidates treat as an afterthought and it causes a surprising share of rejections.

  • Running handwriting, not capital letters. Several notices state this explicitly. A name printed in block capitals is not a signature and can be rejected outright.
  • Black or blue ink on plain white paper. Pencil does not scan cleanly. Ruled paper leaves lines in the image.
  • It must match what you will write later. You will sign the attendance sheet and the answer sheet, and a mismatch at that stage is far more serious than an upload error. Do not invent a fancier signature for the form.
  • Sign inside a box of roughly the right proportion. Draw a light 6 cm × 2 cm rectangle, sign inside it, then crop to the rectangle. This avoids a signature that is a thin line across a wide white field.

For the scan: 200 DPI is enough. Scanning at 600 DPI produces a file far too large for a 10–20 KB band, and compressing it that hard turns the strokes grey and broken.

Why compression makes a signature worse than a photo

JPEG was designed for photographs — smooth gradients of colour. A signature is the opposite: black strokes on white, with hard edges everywhere. Compressed hard, JPEG produces grey halos and speckles around every stroke, and a thin stroke can break into dots.

Two things help. Scan in greyscale rather than colour, which removes two-thirds of the data before compression starts. And do not compress a signature to the very bottom of the band — 14–18 KB in a 10–20 KB range keeps the strokes solid.

A sequence that works

  1. Read your own notice. Note the four numbers: photo dimensions, photo size band, signature size band, and format. Do not rely on last year's figures or on a guide — including this one.
  2. Start from the best original you have. Resizing down preserves detail; enlarging a small photo invents it and looks soft. A 2 MB camera photograph is a good source. A 30 KB WhatsApp forward is not.
  3. Crop to the target ratio. Face centred, a little room above the head, shoulders included.
  4. Resize to the exact pixel dimensions. 200 × 230, or whatever your notice states.
  5. Compress to the middle of the band and check the actual file size in your file manager, not the tool's estimate.
  6. Open the finished file at full size. If the face looks soft or blocky on your own screen, it will look worse on the admit card.
  7. Keep both files with the application number in the filename. You will need them again for the admit card stage, the document verification, and often for a second exam the same season.

Mistakes that cost an application

  • Using a WhatsApp-received photo. WhatsApp recompresses images on send. Starting from an already-compressed file and compressing again produces visible blocking at these sizes. Transfer the original by email, cable or file share.
  • A screenshot of a photograph. A screenshot is the screen's resolution, not the photo's, and the result is soft.
  • Hitting the size by cropping tighter. Cropping out the shoulders to shed kilobytes produces a photo that fails the composition rules instead.
  • Uploading PNG when JPEG is specified. Some portals accept it and then store something the printer cannot handle. Follow the stated format.
  • Leaving it to the last day. Portals are slowest on the closing date, and a rejected upload with two hours left is how applications are lost.

Privacy is worth a thought here

You are handling a photograph of your face and an image of your signature. A signature image is a genuine identity risk — it is the thing used to authorise documents.

Many free resizers upload your file to a server to process it. For an ordinary holiday photo that is a minor matter. For your signature it is not.
