💰 CTC to In-Hand Salary — How to Calculate Your Take-Home Pay

October 2026 · 10 min read

The gap between a CTC figure in an offer letter and the amount that reaches your bank account is routinely 25 to 30 per cent, and almost all of it is predictable. The deductions are rule-based, the rules are published, and the arithmetic is worth doing before you accept an offer rather than after your first payslip.

What CTC actually includes

Cost to Company is what the employer spends on you in a year, which is a different quantity from what you receive.

It typically contains your gross salary plus the employer's own contributions — their share of provident fund, gratuity provisioning, insurance premiums — and sometimes items you never see as money at all, such as a notional value for a meal card or training.

So two offers with identical CTC can pay quite differently, depending on how much of the figure is employer contribution and notional benefit rather than salary.

The structure, top to bottom

LevelWhat it is
CTCEverything the employer spends
GrossCTC minus employer contributions and notional items
Taxable incomeGross minus exemptions and deductions
In-handGross minus PF, tax, professional tax, ESI

The two numbers worth asking about in an interview are gross and the basic, because the basic drives several deductions and allowances.

Provident fund, and the cap that surprises people

PF is 12% of basic salary from the employee and a matching 12% from the employer. The employee share is deducted from your salary; the employer share is part of CTC but never passes through your account.

The detail that matters: the statutory wage ceiling for PF is ₹15,000 of basic. Where an employer applies the ceiling, the contribution is capped at ₹1,800 a month regardless of whether your basic is ₹20,000 or ₹80,000.

Some employers apply PF on the full basic instead, which increases both the deduction and the employer's matching contribution. Both practices exist, so the only way to know is to ask or to read the payslip. It materially changes in-hand pay.

HRA: the three-way calculation

House Rent Allowance is exempt from tax up to the least of three amounts, and people usually assume it is the first.

  1. The actual HRA received.
  2. 50% of basic for metro cities, 40% for non-metro.
  3. Rent paid minus 10% of basic.

The third is the one that binds most often. If your rent is low relative to your basic, the exemption is small however large the HRA component is.

Two further points. From FY 2026-27 the 50% metro list was extended to include Bengaluru, Hyderabad, Pune and Ahmedabad, while FY 2025-26 filings still use the earlier four-city list — so which year you are computing for changes the answer. And landlord PAN is required once annual rent crosses ₹1 lakh.

Professional tax and ESI

Professional tax is levied by the state, so it varies and some states do not charge it at all. Where charged it is a small monthly amount, typically up to ₹200, capped at ₹2,500 a year by statute.

ESI applies only below a gross wage threshold of ₹21,000 a month. Below it, the employee contributes 0.75% of gross and the employer 3.25%. Above it, ESI stops entirely — which is why a small raise across that line can increase in-hand pay by more than the raise itself.

Which tax regime, and why the answer is personal

The New regime has lower slab rates and disallows most deductions. The Old regime has higher rates and allows them. Which is better depends entirely on what you actually claim.

For FY 2025-26 and FY 2026-27 the New regime figures are the same — Budget 2026 left the slab rates and the Section 87A rebate unchanged — with a basic exemption of ₹4 lakh, a standard deduction of ₹75,000 for salaried taxpayers, and a rebate that means no tax up to ₹12 lakh of taxable income.

The practical test: add up what you genuinely claim — 80C, 80D, HRA exemption, home loan interest — and compare. Someone paying significant rent and a home loan often does better on the Old regime; someone with few deductions almost always does better on the New.

Note that a deduction is only worth claiming if you were going to spend the money anyway. An investment made purely to reduce tax has its own cost.

The notional parts of CTC

Items that inflate CTC without reaching you:

  • Gratuity provisioning. Payable only after five years of continuous service. If you leave before that, you never receive it — but it was in the CTC.
  • Employer PF. Real money, in your PF account, not in your bank.
  • Insurance premiums. A genuine benefit, not income.
  • Variable pay and bonus. Often stated at 100% of target in the offer, and paid at whatever the actual performance rating produces.
  • Notional allowances. Meal card value, learning budget, equipment allowance.

The variable component is where offers differ most. A ₹12 lakh CTC with 10% variable is a meaningfully different offer from one with 25% variable, and the difference does not appear in the headline.

Comparing two offers properly

  1. Ask for the gross, not the CTC.
  2. Ask for the basic, because it drives PF and the HRA calculation.
  3. Separate fixed from variable and compare the fixed parts.
  4. Check how PF is applied — on capped ₹15,000 or on full basic.
  5. Compute in-hand under both tax regimes for each offer.
  6. Note the city, which affects both HRA exemption and professional tax.

Comparing CTC to CTC is the one approach that reliably misleads.

Reading your first payslip

Check three things, because errors here persist until someone notices.

The basic matches the offer. Everything else follows from it.

PF is 12% of that basic, or ₹1,800 if the ceiling is applied. A different figure means a different base is being used.

TDS is roughly annual tax divided by remaining months. Early-year TDS is often estimated on assumptions about your declarations, and correcting the declaration early avoids a large deduction later.
