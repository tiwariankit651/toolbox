🎨 Free Online Whiteboard for Teaching and Drawing

October 2026 · 8 min read

A whiteboard works for teaching for a reason that has nothing to do with the technology: the explanation appears at the speed the student can follow it. A finished diagram shows the answer. A diagram being drawn shows the reasoning, and the reasoning is what is being taught.

Why drawing beats showing

A prepared slide presents a completed structure, and the student has to work out which part came first and why. A drawing arrives in order, so the order itself carries information — this depends on that, this is a consequence of this.

It also paces the explanation to the drawing. You cannot draw faster than you can think, which is roughly the speed someone can follow. A slide has no such limit, which is why a dense slide loses the room.

The third effect is attention. Something changing holds attention better than something static, with no effort from the teacher.

What actually helps on a digital board

Features that make a real difference, and ones that do not.

Worth having:

  • A few thick colours. Thin lines are unreadable on a projected or shared screen. Two or three pen widths and four colours cover almost everything.
  • Undo. The most-used control. Being able to retract a line without clearing the board keeps the flow of an explanation.
  • Clear with confirmation. Wiping a board mid-explanation by accident is a real cost.
  • Export as an image. The board is the lesson notes; students should be able to keep it.

Rarely worth it: elaborate shape libraries, templates, dozens of colours. They add decisions at the moment you should be explaining, and a board full of options is slower to use than one with four.

Legible handwriting with a mouse

Writing with a mouse or trackpad is genuinely hard, and most people give up before trying the things that help.

  • Write much larger than feels natural. Mouse movement is coarse, so large letters are smoother. Zoom out afterwards if needed.
  • Move from the elbow, not the wrist. Longer strokes are steadier.
  • Use capitals. Straight lines are far easier than cursive curves with a mouse.
  • Type the labels, draw the structure. If the board has a text tool, use it for anything that needs to be read and keep drawing for arrows, boxes and relationships.

A cheap graphics tablet changes this entirely and is the single best improvement if you teach regularly. A touchscreen with a stylus works well too; a finger on a touchscreen is about as coarse as a mouse.

Teaching on a shared screen

Remote teaching adds constraints that are easy to forget while you can see your own screen.

  • Everything is smaller for them. Someone on a phone sees a fraction of what you see. Write twice as large as you think necessary.
  • Compression blurs thin lines. Video conferencing compresses the screen share, and a one-pixel line can disappear entirely. Use thick strokes.
  • There is a delay. Pause after drawing something before referring to it, because they are a second or two behind.
  • Say what you are drawing. Students on a poor connection may be hearing you clearly and seeing a frozen frame.

Structuring a board so it can be read afterwards

A board that made sense live is often incomprehensible as an exported image, because the spoken part is missing. Three habits fix that at no cost during the lesson.

  1. Put a title on it. One line at the top saying what this board is about.
  2. Number the steps if the explanation had an order. Without numbers, an exported diagram loses its sequence.
  3. Start a new board for a new idea rather than squeezing it into a corner. Two clean boards are far more useful afterwards than one crowded one.

The privacy point, which matters with a class

If you ask students to open an online whiteboard, you are directing them to a third party. When the students are minors, any tool requiring a sign-up is collecting data about children on your instruction — which in many institutions needs consent that has not been obtained.

It is worth checking before sharing a link rather than afterwards. A tool that runs entirely in the browser with no account removes the question instead of answering it.

There is a practical reason too: a board that needs a login is a board that fails when a student cannot log in, in the five minutes before a class.

When a whiteboard is the wrong tool

  • Dense text. If the content is paragraphs, a document is better. A whiteboard is for relationships between things.
  • Anything you will reuse unchanged. Draw it once, export it, present the image next time. Redrawing the same diagram every term is wasted effort.
  • Precise diagrams. A circuit or a chemical structure that must be accurate is better drawn in a dedicated tool.
