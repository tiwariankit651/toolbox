🎯 Random Name Picker and Team Generator

October 2026 · 8 min read

Picking a name at random sounds trivial and has one genuine subtlety: whether a pick can repeat. Getting that wrong is the difference between a fair rotation and a draw where the same person is chosen three times in a row — and both behaviours are correct for different purposes.

With replacement, or without

With replacement means every pick is independent and anyone can be chosen again. Right for a raffle or a prize draw, where each draw should be a fresh chance.

Without replacement means a chosen name is removed. Right for a rotation — who answers next, who presents this week — where repeating defeats the purpose.

Most disappointment with a random picker comes from using the first when you wanted the second. A class rotation that picks the same student twice before reaching half the class is doing exactly what it was asked.

Random does not mean evenly spread

This is the thing worth understanding, because it is why genuinely random results often look broken.

With thirty names and thirty independent picks, you will not get each name once. On average about eleven names are never picked at all, and some are picked three or four times. That is what randomness looks like, and it is unintuitive enough that people assume the tool is faulty.

Humans expect randomness to alternate and spread. Real randomness clusters. If you want an even spread, you do not want random — you want a shuffle.

Shuffling is usually what you actually want

A shuffle produces a random order of everyone, which gives you fairness and coverage at once: every name appears exactly once, in an unpredictable sequence.

For a classroom rotation this is almost always the right tool. Shuffle the class, work down the list, reshuffle when you reach the end. Nobody is asked twice before everyone is asked once, and nobody can predict their turn.

The standard algorithm is Fisher-Yates, which produces every possible ordering with equal probability. A naive approach — sorting with a random comparator — does not, and produces a detectable bias towards certain orderings. Worth knowing if you are implementing it; invisible if the tool does it correctly.

Making teams, and the three kinds of fair

Splitting a list into teams has more than one notion of fairness, and they conflict.

GoalMethodTrade-off
Equal sizesShuffle, then deal round-robinIgnores ability
Balanced abilityRank, then snake draftNeeds a ranking you may not have
Fully unpredictablePure random assignmentSizes and strength may be uneven

Dealing round-robin from a shuffled list is the usual best answer: shuffle once, then assign the first name to team 1, second to team 2, and so on, wrapping around. Sizes differ by at most one, and the composition is random.

With 23 people into 4 teams you get 6, 6, 6, 5 — and the team of 5 should be chosen by the shuffle rather than always being the last, which round-robin from a shuffled list handles automatically.

Where "random" is not random

Three ways a draw stops being fair without anyone intending it.

  • Re-rolling a result you dislike. The moment you reject an outcome and draw again, the process is your preference rather than chance. If some outcomes are unacceptable, remove those names first.
  • Duplicates in the list. A name entered twice has twice the chance. Easy to do when pasting from a spreadsheet with a header row or a trailing blank.
  • Whitespace making near-duplicates. "Ankit" and "Ankit " are two entries. A good tool trims; otherwise check the count matches what you expect.

Being seen to be fair

For anything with a stake — a prize, an assignment nobody wants — the perception matters as much as the method.

Things that help: showing the full list before drawing, so everyone can see who is included; drawing in front of people rather than announcing a result; and stating the rule beforehand, including whether picks can repeat.

If a draw has real consequences, a verifiable method is better than a trusted one. Numbering the list and using a public source of randomness — a lottery number, a dice roll everyone watches — removes the question of whether the tool was honest.

Classroom use

A few specifics that make this work better in practice.

  • Use a shuffle, not repeated picks, so every student is asked before anyone is asked twice.
  • Keep the list between sessions rather than retyping it, and continue where the order left off.
  • Exclude absentees before drawing, so the pick does not land on an empty chair.
  • Consider whether randomness is right at all. For a student who finds being called on stressful, a predictable rotation is kinder and achieves the same coverage.

A privacy note on name lists

A class list, a team roster or a list of participants is personal data about other people — often about children.

Most online pickers send the list to a server. For a list of nicknames that is unimportant; for a class register it is a transfer you may not be permitted to make on your institution's behalf. A browser-based tool keeps the list on your device, which also means it works without a connection.
