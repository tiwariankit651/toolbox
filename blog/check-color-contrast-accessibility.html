🎨 WCAG Colour Contrast Explained

October 2026 · 9 min read

Contrast is the accessibility requirement most often failed and most easily fixed, because it is the one that can be measured exactly. A pair of colours either clears the threshold or it does not, and you can know which before anyone complains.

What trips people up is not the arithmetic. It is that the number depends on things that are not obvious from the design file.

What the ratio measures

Contrast ratio compares the relative luminance of two colours — how much light each reflects — not how different their hues are. It runs from 1:1 for identical colours to 21:1 for pure black on pure white, and nothing can exceed 21:1.

Because hue is irrelevant to the calculation, two colours can look completely different and still fail. Red text on a green background is the classic example: opposite on the colour wheel, similar in luminance, and often below 3:1. Meanwhile two shades of the same blue can pass comfortably if one is much darker.

The thresholds, and why they differ

RequirementRatioApplies to
AA normal text4.5:1Body copy, labels, most interface text
AA large text3:124px and above, or 18.66px bold
AAA normal text7:1Enhanced level, common in government work
AAA large text4.5:1Headings at the enhanced level
Non-text contrast3:1Input borders, icons, focus rings, chart lines

Large text gets a lower threshold because thicker strokes stay legible with less luminance difference. The boundary is literal: 23px regular text needs 4.5:1, and 24px needs only 3:1. One pixel of font size can change whether a colour passes.

The non-text row is the one most often forgotten. A form field whose border is a pale grey at 1.8:1 against white is a real failure — a user who cannot see the boundary cannot see the field.

The mistake that invalidates most checks

People test text against the colour they believe is behind it, rather than the colour actually painted there. Two things break that assumption.

Translucency. A panel at 10% white over a dark page is not white. It is a blend, and its luminance is nowhere near either input. Testing the text against pure white produces a number with no relationship to what is on screen.

Stacking. A badge inside a card inside a section may have three translucent layers between its text and the page background. The effective colour is all of them composited, in order — and the order matters, because compositing is not commutative.

This is the single most common reason a design passes an automated check and still fails in practice.

Gradients have no single background colour

A button filled with a gradient from indigo to pink has a different contrast ratio at every point across it. White text may clear 4.5:1 at the dark end and fall to 3.5:1 at the light end.

The honest test is against the lightest stop when the text is light, and the darkest when the text is dark — that is where the ratio is worst and where the text will actually be hard to read. Testing the midpoint gives a number that is true of a thin band and false either side of it.

Text colours and fill colours are different things

This catches almost everyone with a dark theme.

A colour chosen to be readable as text on a dark background is light. Use that same colour as a fill behind white text and the requirement inverts — it now needs to be dark. A status green tuned for text on a dark card becomes unreadable the moment it is used as a badge background with white label text on it.

The practical answer is two variants of each semantic colour: one tuned as text against your background, one tuned as a fill with light text on top. A single value trying to serve both roles will be mediocre at each.

Contrast is not the whole of colour accessibility

Passing 4.5:1 does not mean the design works for everyone.

  • Colour as the only signal fails regardless of ratio. A red field with no icon and no message is invisible to a user who does not distinguish red from grey. Add a symbol or text.
  • Colour-blind simulation is a separate check. Contrast tells you there is enough light difference; simulation tells you whether the hue difference survives. Red and green at an identical luminance pass neither.
  • Font weight matters and the ratio cannot see it. A 300-weight face at 4.5:1 is harder to read than a 500-weight at the same ratio, because there is less ink on the screen.
  • Ambient light changes everything. A phone in direct sun loses effective contrast; a dim room makes bright text glare.

Fixing a failure without redesigning

There is usually more room than it first appears.

  1. Move one colour, not both. Pushing both towards the middle reduces the ratio. Moving one away from the other increases it.
  2. Keep the hue, change the lightness. This preserves the brand feel while fixing the number, which is an easier conversation than changing the colour.
  3. Give the text its own surface. A message on a solid panel is independent of whatever sits behind the panel, so a later change elsewhere cannot break it.
  4. Raise size or weight. If the text can legitimately be 24px or bold, the threshold drops to 3:1 and a failing colour may now pass.

Checking a whole interface rather than one pair

Testing colour pairs by hand finds the problems you thought to look for. Two things catch the rest.

Test both themes. A colour that works on a dark background frequently fails on a light one, and the reverse. Any value used in both needs checking in both.

Test states, not just the resting view. Error messages, disabled controls, hover and focus styles often use colours nobody checked, because they are not visible until something happens.
