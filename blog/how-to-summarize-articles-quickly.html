📝 How to Summarize Articles Quickly

October 2026 · 9 min read

Summarising is a skill that gets confused with shortening. Cutting an article to a third of its length is easy; producing a third that carries the argument is not. The difference shows up immediately when you try to use your summary a week later and find it tells you what the article was about without telling you what it said.

This guide covers both the manual method and what automated summarising can and cannot do, with the specific failure modes of each.

Two kinds of summary, for two different purposes

Extractive summarising selects sentences from the original and presents them in order. Nothing is invented, so nothing can be wrong that was not already in the text. The result reads as slightly disjointed, because the sentences were written to follow different neighbours.

Abstractive summarising rewrites the content in new words. It reads better and it can be wrong in ways the original was not — a plausible sentence that the source never supported. For study notes and for anything you will cite, extractive is the safer tool.

Most browser-based tools, including ours, are extractive. Large language models are abstractive. Knowing which you are using tells you how much to trust the result.

How extractive summarising actually picks sentences

It is worth knowing, because it explains the characteristic failures.

The usual approach scores each sentence on several signals and keeps the highest-scoring ones: how many of the article's frequent words it contains, whether it sits at the start of a paragraph, whether it contains numbers or named entities, and its length. Sentences that share vocabulary with the rest of the text score highly, on the reasoning that the article's own repeated terms indicate its subject.

That reasoning breaks in predictable ways:

  • A conclusion stated once, in plain words, scores low. The single most important sentence in an article is often the one that uses none of its jargon.
  • Pronouns lose their referent. "This means the policy will fail" is useless when the extracted sentence before it is missing.
  • Contradiction is invisible. If paragraph three says one thing and paragraph nine qualifies it, an extractive summary may keep only the first and present it as the article's position.
  • Repetition wins. A point made three times in different words beats a better point made once.

The manual method, which still beats the tools

For material you need to actually learn, this takes slightly longer and produces something you can use.

  1. Read the whole thing once without marking anything. Highlighting while reading first means highlighting what sounds important rather than what turns out to be.
  2. Find the claim. Most articles are arguing for one thing. Write it in a single sentence, in your own words. If you cannot, you have not understood it yet — which is useful to know.
  3. List what supports it. Usually three to five pieces: a statistic, an example, a mechanism, an authority.
  4. Note what it concedes. The qualifications and counter-arguments are where the actual nuance lives, and they are the first thing every automated summary drops.
  5. Write it closed. Summarise without looking at the article. What you can reproduce is what you have understood; what you cannot, you need to reread.

Step five is the one that matters. A summary written while looking at the source is a copying exercise. Written from memory, it is a comprehension test, and the gaps it reveals are exactly what you should go back and read again.

Length: what actually fits

A useful ratio depends on what the summary is for.

PurposeLengthMust include
Deciding whether to read it1–2 sentencesThe claim only
Revision notes10–15% of the originalClaim, support, qualifications
Citing in an answer2–3 sentences plus the figureThe specific data and its source
Explaining to someone else25–30%Enough mechanism to answer "why"

Below about 10% you are writing a topic label, not a summary. Above about 30% you have not done the work of deciding what matters.

What to keep, specifically

When deciding whether a sentence survives, three things almost always should.

Numbers with their basis. "Spending rose 12%" is incomplete; "spending rose 12% between 2023 and 2025" is usable. A figure without its period or its denominator is not evidence.

Causal claims with their mechanism. "X caused Y" is worth a line only if you also keep how. Otherwise you have memorised an assertion you cannot defend.

The strongest objection. If the article raises a counter-argument and answers it, keep both. In an essay or an interview, knowing the objection is what distinguishes understanding from recall.

Where automated summaries go wrong in practice

Three specific failures worth checking for, because each produces a summary that looks fine.

Dropped negation. A sentence reading "the scheme did not achieve its target" can be extracted as a fragment that reverses the meaning. Rare but severe, and the result is confidently wrong.

Attributed opinion read as fact. An article quoting someone's view produces a sentence stating that view flatly. Your summary now asserts something the article only reported.

Lost hedging. "Early results suggest" becomes "results show". Research writing is full of deliberate qualification and summarising strips it, which turns a tentative finding into a settled one.

The check is quick: after reading a generated summary, go back to the source for any sentence that makes a strong claim and confirm the original was that strong.

Using a tool well

Automated summarising is genuinely useful for triage — deciding which of twenty articles deserves a careful read. It is not a substitute for reading the one that does.

A workflow that uses it for what it is good at:

  1. Summarise everything short, at the one-or-two-sentence level, to decide what to read.
  2. Read the two or three that matter, properly.
  3. Write those summaries by hand, closed-book, because those are the ones you need to retain.

For exam preparation specifically, the hand-written summary is the point. An extracted summary of an editorial you never read will not help you in an interview, because you will not be able to answer the follow-up question.
