💰 Track Monthly Expenses

October 2026 · 9 min read

Almost everyone who starts tracking expenses stops within three weeks. Not because it is hard, but because the usual method asks for more effort than the information is worth: every cup of tea recorded, twenty categories to choose between, and at the end of the month a spreadsheet that tells you what you already knew.

This guide describes a method that survives contact with a real month, and explains the specific numbers worth knowing about your own spending.

Why detailed tracking collapses

The failure is predictable. Day one you record everything. Day four you forget the auto fare. Day six you have three unrecorded cash payments and cannot remember the amounts. Day eight the ledger is wrong, and a ledger you know is wrong is one you stop opening.

The error is treating tracking as accounting. Accounting needs every entry because the total must balance. Tracking needs only enough accuracy to change a decision — and a figure that is 90% right, which you actually have, beats one that would be 100% right if you kept it up, which you will not.

Start with the fixed and the variable

Split spending into two kinds before anything else, because they need completely different handling.

Fixed costs are the same every month and you already know them: rent, EMI, insurance premium, phone plan, subscriptions. These do not need tracking at all. Write them down once; they are a known quantity.

Variable costs move: food, transport, shopping, going out, medical. This is the only part worth recording, and it is where the whole gap between what people think they spend and what they do spend lives.

The first useful number falls straight out of this split: fixed costs as a share of income. Above roughly 50%, you have very little room to respond to anything — a bad month, a medical bill, a job change. That is worth knowing even if you change nothing.

Five categories, not twenty

Every extra category adds a decision at the moment of entry, and decisions are what make you stop. Five is enough to be informative:

  • Food — groceries, eating out, delivery, canteen
  • Transport — fuel, auto, cab, metro, train
  • Bills — anything recurring and unavoidable
  • Personal — clothes, grooming, gadgets, gifts
  • Other — medical, education, repairs, the genuinely unusual

Note that "Other" is doing real work here. The temptation is to add a category whenever something does not fit; resisting that keeps entry fast. If "Other" grows past about 15% of your spending, then split out whatever is filling it.

Record daily, not per transaction

This is the change that makes the difference. Instead of logging each payment, sit down once a day — walking home, or before bed — and enter the day's totals per category. Three entries instead of eleven.

You lose the ability to say which specific purchase was the problem. You gain a ledger you still have on day thirty. For deciding whether food is eating your salary, the daily total is all you need.

If a day is missed, put in an estimate and move on. An estimated day is a small error; an abandoned ledger is a total one.

The numbers worth looking at

After a month you can answer questions you previously guessed at.

NumberHow to read it
Fixed as % of incomeAbove 50% leaves almost no flexibility
Largest variable categoryUsually food; usually larger than expected
Daily average, variable onlyThe figure that makes a single purchase comparable
Worst three daysShows whether overspending is constant or occasional
Income minus everythingWhat is genuinely available to save

The "worst three days" number is the most actionable and the least obvious. If three days account for a third of your variable spending, the problem is specific events — a wedding, a sale, a night out — and nothing about your daily habits needs to change. If spending is flat across the month, the opposite is true. These two situations look identical in a monthly total and call for completely different responses.

Budgeting after you have measured

Setting a budget before you know your own numbers is guessing. After a month of data you can set one that has some chance of holding.

The familiar 50/30/20 split — half to needs, 30% to wants, 20% to savings — is a reasonable starting frame, but it assumes a housing cost that is unrealistic in most Indian cities. If rent alone is 40% of take-home, the arithmetic does not work and forcing it produces a budget you break in week two. Use your measured fixed share as the starting point and divide what is actually left.

One rule is worth following regardless: decide the savings amount first and spend what remains. Saving whatever is left at month end reliably produces nothing, because spending expands to fill the available money.

Cash is where tracking breaks

Digital payments leave a record. Cash does not, and cash spending is systematically under-remembered.

The practical fix is not to record each cash payment. It is to note what you withdraw. Four thousand rupees out of the ATM on the 3rd, and if your pocket is empty on the 12th, that is four thousand of spending in nine days — categorised roughly from memory. Less precise than the digital side, and far better than the nothing you would otherwise have.

Where the data should live

Expense records are unusually sensitive. Your income, your rent, your medical spending and your habits together describe your life in more detail than almost anything else you would type into a website.

A spreadsheet on your own device is private but needs manual sums. A phone app is convenient but usually uploads, and many of them ask for bank access. A browser tool that stores data locally sits in between: nothing is sent anywhere, and the arithmetic is done for you.

Whichever you choose, the question worth asking is where the numbers physically are. "Securely stored" means they are on somebody's server. "In your browser" means they are not.

What a month of data is actually for

Not guilt. The purpose is to make one or two decisions with evidence instead of impression — whether you can afford a commitment, which category to cut if you need to, how much you can genuinely save each month.

Three months is enough to see your real pattern, including the irregular months that a single month hides. After that, tracking can become much lighter: fixed costs are known, and you only need to watch the one or two variable categories that actually move.

"I Don't Know Where Money Goes" Problem

Yeh problem real hai because:

  • UPI payments invisible feel hoti hain — ₹50 here, ₹100 there adds up
  • Subscriptions silently kat jaati hain — Netflix, Spotify, Hotstar, gym, cloud storage
  • Small daily expenses (chai, samosa, auto) feel like nothing individually but total ₹3000-5000/month easily
  • No system = no awareness = no control

Research shows sirf tracking start karne se logon ka spending 10-15% kam ho jaata hai — bina kuch sacrifice kiye. Awareness itself is the intervention.

Method 1: Notebook / Diary

Old school but works. Ek chhoti diary mein daily kharcha likho. Pros: no phone needed, writing makes it stick. Cons: analysis mushkil hai, charts nahi bante, month-end total nikalna tedious.

Method 2: Mobile App

Apps like Walnut, Money Manager, Expense Manager. Pros: categories, graphs, reminders. Cons: most have ads, some need subscription for features, data privacy concerns, multiple apps for multiple people.

Categories to Track — Indian Context

Generic categories (Food, Transport, Entertainment) se better hai India-specific breakdown:

Essential (Needs)

  • Rent / EMI — housing ka kharcha
  • Groceries / Kirana — monthly ration
  • Utilities — electricity, water, gas, internet, phone recharge
  • Transport — petrol, metro, auto/Ola/Uber daily commute
  • Medical — medicines, doctor visits

Lifestyle (Wants)

  • Food outside — Swiggy/Zomato, restaurants, chai-samosa
  • Subscriptions — Netflix, Spotify, Prime, gym, apps
  • Shopping — clothes, gadgets, online purchases
  • Entertainment — movies, outings, games

Savings & Investments

  • SIP / Mutual Funds
  • FD / RD
  • Emergency Fund
  • Insurance premiums

50-30-20 Rule — India Mein Kaise Implement Kare

Simple budget framework:

  • 50% Needs — rent, groceries, transport, utilities, EMIs
  • 30% Wants — eating out, shopping, entertainment, subscriptions
  • 20% Savings — SIP, emergency fund, investments

Example: ₹40,000 salary

  • Needs: ₹20,000 (rent ₹10K + groceries ₹5K + transport ₹3K + utilities ₹2K)
  • Wants: ₹12,000 (food outside ₹4K + shopping ₹4K + subscriptions ₹2K + misc ₹2K)
  • Savings: ₹8,000 (SIP ₹5K + emergency fund ₹3K)

Reality check: Metro cities mein rent alone 40-50% le jaata hai. Toh adjust karo — maybe 60-20-20 ya 50-25-25. Rule ko apni situation ke hisaab se modify karo, lekin savings 15% se neeche mat jaane do.

Common Indian Expense Leaks

Yeh wo expenses hain jo "chhote" lagte hain but monthly total shocking hota hai:

  • Auto/Ola fare — "Bas ₹80 hai" × 30 days = ₹2,400/month. Metro/bus consider karo kuch days
  • Swiggy/Zomato — Average order ₹250-350. Haftey mein 3 baar = ₹3,000-4,000/month. Ghar pe cook karo 2 din extra
  • Chai/coffee outside — ₹30 chai × twice daily × 25 days = ₹1,500. Office mein banao
  • Mobile recharge + data packs — Extra data packs, caller tunes, VAS charges. Check karo
  • Unused subscriptions — Gym nahi jaate? Cancel karo. 3 streaming services? Ek choose karo
  • Convenience fees — Platform fees, delivery charges, surge pricing — chhupa hua kharcha
  • Impulse online shopping — "Sale" pe buying things you don't need

Tracking start karo, 1 month baad yeh leaks clearly dikh jaayenge.

Month-End Review Process

Tracking tabhi kaam karti hai jab review bhi karo. Every month-end (ya 1st of new month), 15 minutes nikalo:

  1. Total spending check karo — income se zyada toh nahi hua?
  2. Category breakdown dekho — kaunsi category highest? Expected tha?
  3. Surprises identify karo — koi unexpected bada kharcha? One-time tha ya recurring hoga?
  4. Leaks spot karo — food delivery kitna gaya? Auto/cab kitna?
  5. Next month ka plan bano — koi category mein reduce karna hai? Savings increase karni hai?

Savings Goals — Motivation Kahan Se Aaye

Abstract "savings" boring lagta hai. Specific goals set karo:

  • Emergency fund: 3 months salary (job loss buffer)
  • Vacation fund: Goa trip ₹15K — 3 months mein ₹5K/month save karo
  • Gadget fund: New laptop ₹50K — 10 months mein ₹5K/month
  • Investment start: First SIP ₹1000/month — grow gradually

Jab goal tangible ho, saving easier feel hoti hai.
