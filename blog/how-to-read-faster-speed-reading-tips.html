📖 How to Read Faster — Speed Reading Tips

October 2026 · 9 min read

Speed reading claims are mostly unsupported, and the useful techniques are the unglamorous ones. The honest position from the research is that reading speed and comprehension trade off against each other beyond a certain point, and the techniques that genuinely help work by removing waste rather than by reading faster.

What the research actually says

Average adult reading speed for comprehension is roughly 200 to 300 words per minute. Claims of 1,000 wpm or more with full comprehension do not survive testing — when comprehension is measured properly, it falls sharply above about 400 to 500 wpm.

The reason is physical as well as cognitive. Reading involves saccades, the small jumps your eyes make, and fixations, the brief pauses where you actually take in text. You can reduce the number of fixations somewhat; you cannot eliminate them, and each one takes a minimum amount of time.

What the famous demonstrations usually involve is skimming — which is a real and valuable skill, and is not reading. The distinction matters because they are useful for different things.

Three habits that genuinely waste time

Regression

Re-reading the line you just read, usually without noticing. Studies of eye movement find this accounts for a substantial fraction of reading time, and much of it is not comprehension failure — it is a habit of distrust.

The fix is a pointer: run a finger or a cursor along the line as you read. It gives your eyes a moving target, and because it only moves forward it makes regression require deliberate effort. This is the single most effective technique in the whole area, and it is the least impressive-sounding.

Subvocalisation

Saying words internally as you read, which caps you at roughly speaking speed.

The usual advice is to suppress it, and that is only partly right. Fully suppressing the inner voice reduces comprehension of difficult material, because the phonological loop is part of how you hold a complex sentence together. What works better is reducing it for easy text and allowing it for hard text — which happens naturally once you stop treating all text at one speed.

Reading everything at one speed

The largest real gain, and it is a decision rather than a skill. A news article and a legal clause do not deserve the same pace, and most people read both at the same rate.

Matching the pace to the purpose

PurposeApproachSpeed
Is this worth reading?Skim: headings, first lines, conclusionVery fast
Find one specific factScan for the keyword onlyVery fast
General understandingNormal reading with a pointer250–400 wpm
Must retain itSlow, with recall afterwards150–250 wpm

Treating the first two as reading is the mistake. Skimming a research paper to decide whether to read it is correct and efficient. Skimming it instead of reading it, and believing you have read it, is not.

Previewing, which costs two minutes and saves ten

Before reading something long, spend two minutes on its structure: the headings, the first sentence of each section, any summary, the figures.

This gives you a map, and reading with a map is substantially faster because you know where the argument is going. You also discover, fairly often, that only two of the eight sections are relevant to you.

For exam preparation this is the highest-return habit in the list. A textbook chapter previewed first reads faster and is retained better than the same chapter read cold.

RSVP and why it tests poorly

Rapid Serial Visual Presentation flashes one word at a time in a fixed position, eliminating eye movement. Several apps use it and the demonstrations are striking.

Research on it finds a specific problem: comprehension suffers, and the main reason is that you cannot regress. Normal reading relies on being able to glance back at the previous clause when a sentence turns out to be structured unexpectedly. RSVP removes that, so a sentence you misparse is simply lost.

It works adequately for simple narrative and poorly for dense material — which is the opposite of what you would want from a tool for studying.

Comprehension is the measure, not speed

Speed without comprehension is not a result. The test is simple and most people skip it: after reading, close the text and write down the main claim and three supporting points.

If you cannot, you did not read it, however fast you went. This check also calibrates your own speed honestly, because the pace at which you can still do it is your real reading speed for that kind of material.

Practising usefully

  1. Measure your baseline on material of the kind you actually read, and test comprehension.
  2. Use a pointer for two weeks. This alone usually produces a measurable gain by removing regression.
  3. Practise at a slightly uncomfortable pace for ten minutes a day, then return to normal. The discomfort is the training.
  4. Deliberately skim things that deserve skimming. Most people read email and news far more carefully than either merits.
  5. Re-measure monthly, with the comprehension check. A speed gain with a comprehension loss is not a gain.

What limits reading speed that no technique fixes

Worth knowing so the effort goes somewhere useful.

  • Unfamiliar vocabulary. You cannot read past a word you do not know. Vocabulary is the real bottleneck for most second-language readers, and no eye-movement technique touches it.
  • Unfamiliar subject. Reading about a field you know is far faster, because you are recognising rather than constructing.
  • Difficult prose. A badly written sentence is slow because it is badly written.
  • Tiredness. Reading speed falls sharply with fatigue, and no technique compensates.

For exam preparation specifically, this means vocabulary work does more for your reading speed than speed-reading practice does.
